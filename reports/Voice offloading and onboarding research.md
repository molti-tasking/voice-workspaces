# Frame Souffleur as learning what to offload

Frame the deployment as **a formative field study of learning to offload**: how people come to hand a voice partner the *holding* of many parallel projects on their commute while keeping the *thinking*, and what it takes to learn that split without a screen. Context switching, the evaluation focus your advisor agreed, is the scenario. Onboarding is the lens that turns your many light users from missing data into findings. The literature supports this framing from three directions:

- **Offloading research** names intentions and open loops as the best-evidenced *beneficial* thing to offload. That holds only when the store is reliable, and it has known memory costs.
- **The 2024–2026 work on generative AI** finds harm almost only when people offload *generation and reasoning*. No study yet separates the two kinds of offloading in everyday use.
- **Every close precedent** (speech-to-canvas tools, in-car LLM agents, reflection agents) was studied seated, in a simulator, on a test track, or in a single session. These searches found no multi-week deployment of an LLM thinking partner on real commutes.

Your data cannot test effects. It does fit a two-level design: one information-rich case of sustained use (36 drives), plus an account across participants of first encounters, light use and non-use. That supports five credible gap claims:

1. naturalistic commute deployment;
2. eyes-free capture with review deferred to the desk;
3. ambiguous silence;
4. learning an agent's capabilities without a screen;
5. observing where relieving what people hold slides into relieving them of the thinking.

It rules out claims about efficacy, driving safety, change over time, or whether "declined offers never return" works.

The final interviews are now the critical path. They have to recover what the instruments missed:

- whether captured items came back, which the empty day-7 review never recorded;
- the difference between "didn't learn", "didn't need" and "didn't trust";
- how people read the agent's silences.

## Eleven people, one deep case: what your data can carry

Between September and October 2026, 11 people outside the team recorded at least one drive. Together they made **82 drives and about 6.6 hours** of talk. How those drives are spread matters more than the totals:

- **One heavy user** made 36 drives (about 4.5 hours) over 16 active days, with 13 debriefs, 48 archive or curation actions and both macro proposals.
- **Four others** used it for real but lightly: 1–9 drives and 12–33 minutes each.
- **One guest** made 20 drives with a median length of about 18 seconds, which reads as repeated false starts.
- **The remaining five** left only traces.
- **Your own account** (60 drives) is not participant data.

Read this way, the corpus is mostly about first encounters, plus one deep case of what sustained use can look like. Each group answers a different question.

| Group | Who | What they did | What they can tell you | Final interview |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sustained use | 1 heavy user | 36 drives, ~4.5 h, 16 active days, 13 debriefs, 48 curation actions, 2 macro proposals (neither decided) | Context switching, what came back, ownership, macros, over-offloading, early vs. late use | ≈70 min |
| Light use | 4 people | 1–9 drives, 12–33 min each | First encounters, calibration, limiting vs. leaving, didn't learn / need / trust | ≈40 min |
| Failed onboarding | 1 guest | 20 drives, median ~18 s | Whether it was a learning failure or a technical failure | ≈20 min |
| Traces | 5 people | At least one drive, too little to count as use | Why it never got going | 10-min call, if willing |
| Researcher | you | 60 drives | First-person design data, reported separately | n/a |

Report the in-the-moment ratings as raw values per participant, never as means ([Dragicevic 2016](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-319-26633-6_13), from memory). Ratings exist for only **6 drives from 5 participants**, but they still say something:

- **"Could you tell it was still working?"** scored **1–2.5 for three of four respondents**. That matches the 40-second silences in your two pilot write-ups. It is the most consistent signal across all your sources.
- **"Could you correct it?"** scored 1, 4.5 and 7. A spread that wide suggests some people found how to correct it and others never did. That is a finding about learning the system, not a satisfaction score.
- **"Did it do thinking you wanted to do?"** scored 1, 5 and 1. Two of three respondents felt the agent stayed on the right side of your principle and one did not. That one person is the most important follow-up in the interviews.
- **"Did talking move your thinking on?"** scored 4, 5 and 4. Read this with care. A meta-analysis of 94 studies found that plain thinking aloud has essentially no effect on performance (r = −.03), while being asked to explain does change it ([Fox et al. 2011](https://supp.apa.org:443/psycarticles/supplemental/a0021663/a0021663_supp.html), secondary only). The interview has to separate whether speaking aloud or the agent's replies moved people on.

The instruments failed at exactly the moments the design is about: arriving at the end of a drive, and coming back to the desk.

- **22 of the heavy user's 36 drives ended by idle timeout rather than Stop**, so no debrief or rating followed. The ratings and debriefs you have therefore over-represent drives where someone ended the session on purpose. State that bias.
- **The day-7 review (done / open / lost per captured item) has zero rows.** Whether captured items came back can now only be reconstructed in the interviews.
- Nobody submitted the free-text survey, and nobody has a pseudonym yet.

The roughly 23 spoken debriefs (13 from the heavy user) are your strongest in-the-moment qualitative data. Frame them as voice-mail diary entries, an established method for mobile, hands-busy settings ([Palen & Salzman 2002](https://doi.org/10.1145/587078.587092)). Usefully, the debrief's three fixed questions already map onto the research questions recommended below:

- "What couldn't it do?" captures capabilities people tried (learning).
- "What did it do unasked?" captures where it may have taken over thinking.
- "What would you make into a thing?" captures what people want held.

Four pieces of housekeeping will decide how reviewers read everything else.

1. **Separate your own drives.** Exclude your 60 drives from participant data. If your own use shaped the design, report it separately as autobiographical design ([Neustaedter & Sengers 2012](https://doi.org/10.1145/2317956.2318034), from memory; [Desjardins et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1145/3492342)).
2. **Call it a research prototype.** The system changed during the deployment, so it is not a "technology probe": probes by definition "are not changed during the use period" ([Hutchinson et al. 2003](https://doi.org/10.1145/642611.642616)). Publish a dated change log next to the usage timeline, because novelty effects come back "when changes are made to the system's state" ([Koch et al. 2018](https://dl.eusset.eu/handle/20.500.12015/3115)). A month-long voice-assistant study that switched to an LLM partway through was published once it stated the change openly ([Mahmood et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1145/3796236)).
3. **Present the two pilots as Study 0.** Their failures (40-second silences, an agent claiming actions it had not taken) motivated specific changes. CHI has accepted staged designs that end in a field stage with only five participants ([Mahmood et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1145/3706598.3713839)).
4. **Treat the 22-hour recording as an incident.** Check with the participant, follow your ethics protocol on deletion, and leave it out of talk-time totals.

None of this makes the study weak by HCI standards, as long as it claims the right thing. Empirical contributions "are evaluated mainly on the importance of their findings and on the soundness of their methods" ([Wobbrock & Kientz 2016](https://doi.org/10.1145/2907069)). Demonstrating effects "is often infeasible and unnecessary" for early-stage or novel technologies ([Klasnja et al. 2011](https://doi.org/10.1145/1978942.1979396)).

The most common CHI sample size is 12 ([Caine 2016](https://doi.org/10.1145/2858036.2858498)). You are below that, so argue from **information power**: a narrow aim, a very specific sample, established theory and case-by-case analysis all reduce how many participants you need ([Malterud et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1177/1049732315617444)). Precedents include:

- a three-week voice-assistant deployment with seven participants, published at CSCW as "a preliminary assessment" ([Pradhan et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1145/3359316));
- a 14-day study of a proactive agent with 12 participants at CHI 2026 ([Abbas et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1145/3772318.3790957)).

Field-trial methodologists warn that results often hinge "on the insights of a subset of trial participants" ([Brown et al. 2011](https://doi.org/10.1145/1978942.1979185)). Reviewers expect you to name the heavy-user skew; hiding it is what hurts. Avoid the word "longitudinal". With 1–9 drives, the light users' data comes almost entirely from the novelty phase, so "formative" and "early adoption" describe it honestly ([Kjærup et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-030-67322-2_2)).

## Offloading research backs "hold, don't think" as a bet with known costs

**The holding half of your principle has strong theoretical backing.** Cognitive offloading means using a physical action to reduce mental demand. People decide whether to offload based on beliefs about their own abilities, not on what would be optimal ([Risko & Gilbert 2016](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tics.2016.07.002), from memory).

The best-evidenced helpful case is **intention offloading**. Calendars, reminders and placed objects are "highly effective" for carrying out delayed intentions, yet much less is known about them than about remembering intentions unaided ([Gilbert et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9971128/)). A field study can speak to that gap.

The mechanism you are betting on has direct experimental support. Saving one file before studying another improved memory for the new file, but **only when saving was reliable** ([Storm & Stone 2015](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797614559285)). An external store counts as part of someone's thinking only when it is reliably available and trusted ([Clark & Chalmers 1998](https://doi.org/10.1093/analys/58.1.7), from memory). A 2025 *Nature Communications* perspective frames the design question for generative AI as how to integrate it, not whether to avoid it ([Clark 2025](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-025-59906-9)). Reliability is therefore a precondition of your principle, not a nice-to-have. The pilot agent that claimed actions it had not taken undermines exactly that precondition.

**The same literature says relieving people of holding is not free.**

- More offloading improved immediate performance but reduced memory in a surprise test ([Grinschgl et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1177/17470218211008060)).
- People prefer external reminders even when it costs them money, a bias driven by underconfidence in their own memory ([Gilbert et al. 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31448938/)).
- A 2026 study reports that once reminders were removed, former reminder users remembered fewer future tasks than people who had never used them ([Fellers & Storm 2026](https://www.psypost.org/relying-on-reminders-could-be-weakening-your-memory-skills/), secondary only).

The end of your deployment is such a withdrawal. Present the principle as a bet with known costs, and use the interviews to ask each participant what they would lose track of without Souffleur.

**For context switching, steer away from the folk idea that unfinished tasks "nag".** A 2025 meta-analysis found **no memory advantage for unfinished tasks** (recall ratio about 0.99). It did find a robust tendency to *go back to* unfinished tasks, known as the Ovsiankina effect ([Ghibellini & Meier 2025](https://doi.org/10.1057/s41599-025-05000-w)). Switching between parallel projects is going back to them. The partner's job is to hold each project's "where was I" so that going back is cheap.

A second finding sharpens the principle. Unfinished goals caused intrusive thoughts and worse performance on an unrelated task. **Making a specific plan removed those effects, even though the goal was still unfinished** ([Masicampo & Baumeister 2011](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0024192), secondary only; the notes found no replication record). If that holds, the relief comes from the person stating the next step, which is itself a small act of thinking. That reconciles the two halves of your principle: the partner holds the project's state and asks for the next step rather than writing it.

The commute is already where this kind of thinking happens:

- Drivers on familiar commutes mind-wander about two-thirds of the time ([Burdett et al. 2019](https://researchcommons.waikato.ac.nz/handle/10289/12097)).
- Across three field studies with 1,736 participants, thinking ahead about the upcoming work role buffered the costs of long commutes ([Jachimowicz et al. 2021](https://www.repository.cam.ac.uk/handle/1810/302690)).
- A conversational agent supporting start-of-day and end-of-day transitions changed information workers' behaviour over 14 days ([Williams et al. 2018](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/?p=465405)).

Capturing thoughts is not enough on its own. Capture systems fail at retrieval and use ([Sellen & Whittaker 2010](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/?p=159460)). In task management, reminding at the right moment matters more than keeping the list ([Bellotti et al. 2004](https://doi.org/10.1145/985692.985785), from memory). That is why the empty day-7 review hurts, and why the interviews must recover what it should have recorded.

**The thinking half rests on the 2024–2026 evidence about generative AI.** That evidence converges on one pattern: when AI does the generating or reasoning, the output gets better and the person engages less.

- In a survey of 319 knowledge workers, **higher confidence in generative AI went with less critical thinking**. Their critical work shifted toward checking outputs and "task stewardship" ([Lee et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1145/3706598.3713778)).
- Students using ChatGPT wrote better essays without learning more ([Fan et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1111/bjet.13544)).
- Students using an LLM reported lower mental load but produced weaker reasoning. So **lower workload cannot stand in for preserved thinking** ([Stadler et al. 2024](https://opus.bibliothek.uni-augsburg.de/opus4/frontdoor/index/index/docId/114673)).
- In a field experiment with about 1,000 students, those given plain GPT-4 did 17% worse than controls once access was removed. A version with learning safeguards largely avoided that harm ([Bastani et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2422633122)).

The labels "cognitive debt" ([Kosmyna et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.08872), preprint, critiqued in [Stanković et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.00856), preprint) and "cognitive surrender" ([Shaw & Nave 2026](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=6097646), preprint, numbers secondary only) are provocative but not established. Cite them as concepts.

**Your principle sits in a gap between these two literatures.**

- Every harm study in the notes tested offloading of generation or reasoning: essays, inquiry, problem solving, coding, decisions.
- None tested offloading intentions or open loops to an LLM. Even a 2026 reflection tool defines problematic AI offloading as substituting LLM output for "reasoning, idea generation, learning, or communication" ([Alam 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.13155), preprint).
- The memory literature, in turn, shows costs to memory for the offloaded items, not to reasoning.

**"Offload holding, keep thinking" is consistent with both literatures and tested by neither.** That makes it a credible hypothesis for a formative study to examine, not a result to claim.

The boundary leaks in three places in Souffleur, and the paper should name them:

1. **Extraction and summaries are synthesis.** Across seven experiments, learning from an LLM summary produced shallower knowledge than learning from web search ([Melumad & Yun 2025](https://doi.org/10.1093/pnasnexus/pgaf316)).
2. **Drafts are generation.** People do not feel they own AI-written text, yet they still call themselves its author ([Draxler et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.03283)).
3. **Proactive offers** are the moments where the user can take on the agent's framing without noticing.

Ownership deserves particular attention. In a preregistered study with 470 participants, **72.8% of people with self-written goals had acted on several of them after two weeks, against 46.6% of people with LLM-written goals**. Ownership explained every downstream outcome ([Chi et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.12344), preprint). If Souffleur rewrites a spoken intention into a polished task, it may cost follow-through. Keep the user's own words attached to each extracted task.

The available countermeasures fit your stance:

- AI that asked questions improved people's ability to spot flawed reasoning more than AI that gave explanations ([Danry et al. 2023](https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/150613)).
- People detected inaccuracies better in static text than in a conversation ([Anderl et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-024-67829-6)). That supports deferring checking to the desk.

**Four claims would draw justified criticism:**

1. That offloading holding is cost-free (contradicted by Grinschgl; Fellers & Storm).
2. That the Zeigarnik effect makes open loops nag (contradicted by Ghibellini & Meier).
3. That lower workload shows thinking was preserved (contradicted by Stadler).
4. That people "remember where to find something, not what it was" as settled fact. The classic Google-effects result failed to replicate ([Camerer et al. 2018 replication record](https://forrt.org/flora-replication-atlas/doi/10.1126/science.1207745/)).

## No prior study puts a thinking partner on real commutes

**The nearest systems turn speech into structure, but always with the structure on screen and the user seated.**

- **Orality** (CHI 2026) uses an LLM to turn continuous speech into a node-link map. In a 20-minute lab study with 12 participants, self-rated clarity gains were not significantly different from ChatGPT plus dictation. Eight of 12 still preferred Orality, and the most valued feature was the **on-demand** "Ask me questions" ([Li et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.02544v1)).
- **Rambler** extracts the gist of dictated text ([Lin et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.10838)). Its 7–10-day diary study found writers used it mostly sitting at desks or in bed ([Yang et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1145/3706599.3706676)).
- **PointAloud** names users' lack of "awareness of what is captured" as a core problem of capturing think-aloud speech ([Gmeiner et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.09296v1)).
- **Memoro** tested a proactive wearable memory assistant in the lab ([Zulfikar et al. 2024](https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/155181)).

Souffleur differs on all four shared traits: no screen during capture, agent-initiated turns, weeks of use, and a moving vehicle. Orality's three "Lazy Talkers" out of twelve may be the lab version of your light users, who used Souffleur mainly as somewhere to dump dictation.

**In-car research is either about safety or done in simulation.** The key earlier work is a CHI 2019 simulator study with 28 participants on speech-based productivity while driving ([Martelaro et al. 2019](https://microsoft.com/en-us/research/uploads/prod/2019/02/productivity-in-the-car.pdf)):

- Driving held up, but drivers' awareness of their surroundings dropped.
- Support that "inhibited their flow of thought" made things worse.
- One participant asked for exactly your design: "If I could do nothing more than talk and organize my thoughts in an 'outline' … that would be really useful."

Souffleur is the real-world successor to that paper and should cite it prominently. Recent LLM in-car work is one of three kinds:

- on real roads but with scripted tasks: Gemini Live conversations at roughly the mental load of a hands-free phone call ([Monk et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.15034v1), preprint);
- in a simulator ([Bond et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.08101), preprint);
- on a test track, reporting three distinct user profiles ([Cockram et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.25421v1)).

The safety evidence calls for modesty. Voice-based in-vehicle systems imposed **moderate to high mental workload (mean 3.34 of 5). "Practice did not eliminate the interference", and costs lingered for 27 seconds after an interaction ended** ([Strayer et al. 2015, AAA report](https://aaafoundation.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/MeasuringCognitiveDistractionintheAutomobileIIIReport.pdf)). US guidance covers only tasks that need the driver's eyes and hands ([NHTSA 2016](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2016-12-05/html/2016-29051.htm)). You measured nothing about driving, so make no safety claims. Describe the hands-free, no-screen design and cite other studies' workload data.

**Reflection and planning agents have been deployed in the field, but never voice-first in a car.**

- A 2018 IMWUT reflection agent ran for two weeks with 33 Fitbit users. Sixteen chose to keep using it for two more weeks without pay ([Kocielnik et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1145/3214273)). That is a behavioural sign of value worth copying by offering continued access.
- The same group's workplace reflection agent already added a separate voice channel alongside chat ([Kocielnik et al., DIS 2018](https://doi.org/10.1145/3196709.3196784); participant count unverified). So do not claim the first voice reflection agent.
- The closest current template is the CHI 2026 study of a proactive planning and reflection agent: 12 participants over 14 days. Participants accepted, negotiated, resisted or disengaged. **Rigidity, premature turn-taking and overpromising** drove the resistance ([Abbas et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1145/3772318.3790957)).

**Your most robust finding, that people could not tell whether the agent was still working, has a clear home in the literature, with a new twist.**

- Voice interaction has to keep its own state clear to the user while the user is busy with something else ([Porcheron et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1145/3173574.3174214)).
- "Non-answer responses impede progress", while a brief account of the delay lets the conversation recover ([Fischer et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1145/3342775.3342788)).
- With LLM agents, delays beyond **about 4 seconds degraded the experience. Natural spoken fillers helped; generic wait indicators did not** ([Maslych et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.22352v1)).
- Moderate delays can even read as the AI deliberating, until long waits tip into "concerns about reliability" ([Tan et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.06183)).
- Research on agent communication lists "What is the agent currently doing?" as a core open challenge ([Bansal et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.10380), preprint).

The twist: this literature treats silence as response delay in a request-and-answer exchange. When someone thinks aloud, silence has four possible meanings: **you are thinking, the agent is listening, the agent is working, or the system has failed.** A driver cannot glance at a screen to tell which. The notes found no study of this "ambiguous silence", so the paper can own it as a central concept.

**Proactive offers that support thinking, rather than navigation or safety, have little evidence behind them.** A systematic review of 21 studies of proactive voice assistants found clear benefits mainly in safety-critical and emergency situations ([Bérubé et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chbr.2024.100411)). Your advisor's "offer, never assign" rule has good support:

- In a smart-home study, asking the user beat assuming what they wanted ([Oh et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1145/3613904.3642193)).
- Cautious proactive strategies built trust; fully autonomous behaviour did not ([Kraus et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1145/3340631.3394840)).
- In a driving simulator, people accepted **73.8% of proactive suggestions, rising to 82.5% for driving-relevant ones** ([Schmidt et al. 2020](https://aclanthology.org/2020.lrec-1.61/)). Expect a lower rate for offers about thinking.

Two preprints matter for context switching:

- In real AI-assisted work, **the model starting a switch to a different task was the strongest predictor of decline** ([Lepine et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.10742), preprint). An offer that pulls a driver into another project probably costs more than one that deepens the current one.
- Even a model trained for the purpose was only about 66% accurate (F1) at judging when to offer help ([Lu et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.12361), preprint). So declines will be common.

The notes found **no study of what happens when a declined offer is, or is not, repeated**. With two undecided macro proposals, you cannot test "declined offers never return" either. Present it as a design contribution grounded in this literature, not as a finding.

Two more design implications follow:

1. **Give a one-clause reason with each offer.** In automated driving, explanations given after an action did not build trust the way explanations given before it did ([Du et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.trc.2019.05.025)).
2. **Defer correction to the desk on purpose.** In the original human-AI guidelines study, voice assistants drew the most "does not apply" ratings. Their one success at letting users correct the AI depended on falling back to a screen ([Amershi et al. 2019](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2019/01/Guidelines-for-Human-AI-Interaction-camera-ready.pdf)). That is a precedent for deliberately deferring correction to the desk.

## Onboarding turns light users from missing data into findings

Most of your participants never got past their first weeks, so most of your data is about first encounters. An onboarding lens describes what you actually observed. It also changes what the light users mean:

- **Without the lens**, four light users and a false-start guest are missing data to apologise for.
- **With it**, they are evidence about how people learn, or fail to learn, what an eyes-free partner can hold.

Non-use is worth analysing in its own right ([Satchell & Dourish 2009](https://doi.org/10.1145/1738826.1738829), from memory). It takes forms such as limiting, leaving and relapsing ([Baumer et al. 2013](https://doi.org/10.1145/2470654.2466446), from memory), and drop-out deserves study rather than being glossed over ([Eysenbach 2005](https://doi.org/10.2196/jmir.7.1.e11)).

**Research on voice assistants from before LLMs maps closely onto what you saw.**

- Users' expectations were "dramatically out of step" with what the systems could do: a gap between what users try to do and what they can see the system doing ([Luger & Sellen 2016](https://doi.org/10.1145/2858036.2858288)).
- A classification of obstacles separates requests the system does not support, recognition errors, missing or unclear feedback, and system errors. Users preferred guessing to looking for help ([Myers et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1145/3173574.3173580); details secondary only). In those terms, "is it still working?" is a feedback failure, and asking Souffleur to stop by voice was an unsupported request.
- Older adults abandoned Echo devices because of "difficulty finding valuable uses" ([Trajkova & Martin-Hammond 2020](https://doi.org/10.1145/3313831.3376760)).
- Memory features such as reminders were barely used "due to reliability concerns" ([Pradhan et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1145/3373759)).
- Home voice agents faded as they lost their "presence" ([Cho et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1145/3322276.3322332)). Souffleur has no physical presence at all; it exists only when a drive starts.
- Across about 110 days of Google Home logs, people tried new phrasings, but "exploration of new topics was low" ([Bentley et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1145/3264901)).
- In a lab comparison, both proactive capability prompts and help on request beat offering neither. People preferred help on request for frequent use, but nobody has tested how that preference shifts over weeks ([Kirschthaler et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1145/3405755.3406119)).

**LLMs move the problem rather than remove it.** People can now say almost anything, but what the agent can actually do and hold is still narrow and invisible.

- The "gulf of envisioning" describes users not knowing what the task should be, how to instruct the model, or what to expect back ([Subramonyam et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1145/3613904.3642754)).
- With generative tools, people skip tutorials, and "the prompt box became the entry point for learning, collapsing onboarding into immediate action" ([Gautam et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1145/3772318.3791124)). For Souffleur, the open microphone is the prompt box, and the first drive is the onboarding.
- Across 140,000 chat sessions, people's interaction patterns settled quickly, and whether they kept using the tool correlated with how much they explored early on ([Zhu et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.05767), preprint).

Two findings explain the pilot agent claiming actions it had not taken:

- With an LLM voice assistant, "LLMs absorb the majority of VA intent recognition failures" ([Mahmood et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijhcs.2024.103406)). A fluent reply hides the fact that nothing happened.
- People overestimate how accurate LLMs are, especially when given longer explanations ([Steyvers et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1038/s42256-024-00976-7)).

The risk is therefore not only that people under-use the agent, but that they over-trust what it holds. In-car voice interaction also stayed demanding after a week of practice ([Strayer et al. 2015](https://aaafoundation.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/MeasuringCognitiveDistractionintheAutomobileIIIReport.pdf)). Learning what the agent can do therefore probably belongs outside the drive: on the board before and after, with only short answers on request in the car.

**This is what ties onboarding and offloading into a single framing.**

- The decision to offload depends on people's beliefs about their own abilities compared with the aid ([Risko & Gilbert 2016](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tics.2016.07.002), from memory).
- Appropriate reliance requires trust that matches what the system can actually do ([Lee & See 2004](https://doi.org/10.1518/hfes.46.1.50_30392), from memory).
- In a study of onboarding to medical AI, pathologists wanted to know up front the model's "known strengths and limitations", and likened this to forming a mental model of a colleague ([Cai et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1145/3359206)).
- Setting expectations before first use measurably raised acceptance of an imperfect AI ([Kocielnik et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1145/3290605.3300641)).

People can only hand off what they believe the partner will hold, and they learn that belief from how it behaves. **Onboarding is learning what to offload.** That phrase lets one paper carry both stories without becoming two papers.

**The notes found no validated way to tell "didn't learn it" from "didn't need it".** Build the distinction by comparing several sources:

- **Didn't learn:** the participant tried things that failed (their answers to "what couldn't it do?" record these attempts), or, shown a list, recognises capabilities they would have valued but never knew about.
- **Didn't need:** no attempts, and commutes without much on their mind.
- **Didn't trust:** they kept a parallel list, or never relied on it to hold anything important. This echoes Pradhan's reliability finding.

The false-start guest is an especially informative case. Twenty attempts show motivation. The question is whether those 18-second drives were a failure to learn the basics on first use ([Grossman et al. 2009](https://doi.org/10.1145/1518701.1518803), from memory) or a technical fault, and only the participant can say.

The two undecided macro proposals have a double role worth examining. A proposal offers automation, but it also reveals that a capability exists. Research on recommending software commands recognises this discovery function ([Matejka et al. 2009](https://doi.org/10.1145/1622176.1622214), from memory; [White 2018](https://doi.org/10.1145/3185336)), but it has never been studied by voice.

**Expect one objection: these are well-known problems of discovering what a voice interface can do.** The defence has two parts:

- **What is specific to LLM agents:** fluent replies that hide failed actions; proactive offers and macros that also teach capabilities; and learning what the agent *holds*, rather than which phrasing it understands.
- **The setting:** people cannot learn by seeing on screen what an action will do.

Frame the onboarding contribution as "breakdowns and design implications in the first weeks", not as a theory of how learning unfolds. Showing how learning develops over time would need more than one sustained user. Raise onboarding with your advisor as an addition to the agreed context-switching focus, not a replacement for it: context switching is what the heavy user did, and onboarding explains why the others did not.

## The final interviews must recover what the instruments missed

**Run the final conversations as data-prompted interviews.** Show each participant only their own metadata timeline: drive dates, lengths, how each drive ended, and debrief dates. Personal data like this is used to "stimulate … rich narratives anchored in personal experiences" ([Kwasnicka et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1037/hea0000234)).

- **To get depth from few drives,** take people back to one specific drive with detailed, moment-by-moment questions ([Hogan et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1109/TVCG.2015.2511718)). Ask for critical incidents: the drive where it helped most, and the drive where it most got in the way ([Flanagan 1954](https://doi.org/10.1037/h0061470)).
- **To keep the privacy promise intact,** let participants control the screen. They open their workspace, look, and tell you only the verdict unless they choose to show more. This makes the session data donation filtered by the participant ([Boeschoten et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.5117/CCR2022.2.002.BOES)), with participants interpreting their own intimate data ([Gomez Ortega et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1145/3546155.3546646)).
- **To limit the urge to please you,** remember that you built Souffleur. Respondents were about **2.5 times more likely to prefer an artefact they believed the interviewer had built** ([Dell et al. 2012](https://doi.org/10.1145/2207676.2208589)). Ask about incidents rather than for evaluations. Weight behaviour, such as who keeps using it when offered continued access, over stated satisfaction. If possible, have a colleague co-run the heavy user's interview.

**The revision keeps every section of your draft guide and makes eight changes:**

1. It opens with the participant's own timeline.
2. It adds a block on the first drive and on what participants think Souffleur can do.
3. It asks how participants read silence, using its four possible meanings.
4. It asks for unaided recall *before* the workspace is opened, then asks about the source and ownership of each item.
5. It adds "never mine" and "would have remembered anyway" to the day-7 verdicts.
6. It adds a block on how drives ended.
7. It adds a non-use card sort for light users and the false-start user.
8. It ends with a question about losing Souffleur, plus a re-rating of the four post-drive items in hindsight. That gives every interviewee one comparable set of values; report them as retrospective.

## Recommendation: one framing, three questions, nineteen days

**What changed in working through the literature is the status of your weakest data.**

- **The uneven participation** looks like a failed deployment, but it is the normal shape of voice-assistant adoption. Read through onboarding, it is evidence.
- **The instruments that came back empty** failed at the same two moments the design is about: arriving at the end of a drive, and returning to the desk a week later. That is a finding about systems that span the car and the desk, as much as a gap in the study.
- **Your design principle** sits in an unclaimed gap between two literatures, each of which supports one half of it.
- **The strongest single finding, ambiguous silence,** is already in hand from the pilots and the ratings.

The interviews now need to deliver two things: whether captured items came back, and the split between didn't learn, didn't need and didn't trust.

I considered and rejected three alternatives:

- **A pure offloading-effects paper.** Your data cannot show relief or preserved thinking.
- **A pure onboarding paper.** It would drop the focus your advisor agreed and the heavy user's rich case.
- **A paper auditing the Amershi human-AI guidelines.** That needs a different study.

The framing below uses material from all three without becoming any one of them.

**The framing.** A formative field study of learning to offload: how people come to hand a voice thinking partner the holding of many parallel projects on their commute while keeping the thinking, and what it takes to learn that split without a screen. Working title: *Learning What to Hand Off: A Formative Field Study of a Voice Thinking Partner for Context Switching on the Commute.*

**RQ1 (Holding).** When people think aloud about many parallel projects on the commute, what do they hand the partner to hold? Does it come back at the desk in a form that helps them switch into a project, and that they still own?

**RQ2 (Thinking).** Where do people experience the partner's help (extraction and summaries, drafts, proactive offers, macros) as relieving what they hold, and where as taking over thinking they wanted to do?

**RQ3 (Learning).** How do people learn, while driving and without a screen, what the partner can hold and do? What separates those who did not learn it, did not need it, or did not trust it?

**Contribution statement.** "We report a formative field deployment of Souffleur, a voice thinking partner that people used on their own car commutes. Souffleur captures spoken thinking, structures it into a workspace reviewed later at a desk, and talks back with drafts and proactive offers. Through one in-depth case of sustained use and an account of early adoption and non-use across all participants, we contribute:

1. an account of what people hand off to hold when juggling parallel projects, and whether it comes back in a form they still own;
2. an analysis of where the partner's help crossed from relieving what people hold into doing thinking they wanted to do, locating that boundary in summaries, drafts and offers;
3. an account of how people learn, without a screen, what such a partner can hold and do, identifying *ambiguous silence* and fluent replies that hide failed actions as the breakdowns that stall this learning;
4. design implications for eyes-free thinking partners: silence that explains itself, offers that come with a reason and never return once declined, checking deferred to the desk, and teaching capabilities outside the drive. We also describe a study design for observing intimate spoken thinking without anyone reading or hearing it."

For IMWUT, foreground the car-to-desk pipeline and the privacy-preserving instrumentation as a secondary system contribution. Do not claim that Souffleur reduces mental load, improves productivity or follow-through, is safe to use while driving, or changes behaviour over time. Do not claim that "declined offers never return" works.
