# Small-n Formative Field Deployments in HCI/UbiComp: Methods, Framing, and Acceptance at IMWUT/CHI

Notes for framing the "Souffleur" formative field study: a voice AI thinking partner used on car commutes. About 6 external participants over about 4 weeks. One heavy user (36 drives); most made 1–9 drives. Data: counts-only logs, PostHog, 6 ratings from 5 people, about 23 spoken debriefs, planned interviews, and 2 pilot write-ups. Target: IMWUT, 1 Nov 2026.

Labels: **[Foundational]** = methods or classic papers from before 2022. **[Current]** = 2022–2026. Every DOI was checked against Crossref metadata. Each paper's finding comes from its abstract or full text where I could get it; I say so when I could not.

---

## 1. Accepted contribution types, how small-n deployments argue their value, and typical sample sizes and durations

### Takeaway
HCI venues accept "empirical study of system use" and "understanding users" contributions on their own terms. These are judged on how important the findings are and how sound the method is, not on statistical power. Small-n deployments argue their value through three routes: depth of understanding, the "formative / probe / early-stage" logic (proving efficacy is neither feasible nor needed), and in-the-wild validity. For context, the most common CHI sample size is 12, and 2–4-week deployments are a normal duration bracket. About 6 participants over about 4 weeks is at the low end for N but normal for duration. So the paper must win on depth, honest scoping and information power, not on breadth.

### Cited Findings

**Contribution typologies and venue criteria**
- **[Foundational] Wobbrock, J.O. & Kientz, J.A. (2016). "Research contributions in human-computer interaction." *ACM Interactions* 23(3).** [doi:10.1145/2907069](https://doi.org/10.1145/2907069). Defines seven contribution types: empirical, artifact, methodological, theoretical, dataset, survey and opinion. On empirical contributions: they "are evaluated mainly on the importance of their findings and on the soundness of their methods." On artifacts: they "are often accompanied by empirical studies but do not have to be, and sometimes should not be". Systems are "evaluated in a holistic fashion according to what they make possible and how they do so." CHI 2016 data in the article: "empirical study of system use" was 44.0% of submissions and was accepted at 23.5%, against 23.6% overall. "Empirical study of people" was 28.4% of submissions and had the highest acceptance rate, 26.3%. About 30% of submissions declared more than one contribution type. *Implication:* frame Souffleur as a primary empirical contribution (an empirical study of system use) with a secondary artifact contribution, and state both explicitly.
- **[Current] CHI 2026 Papers Committee. "Contributions to CHI."** [chi2026.acm.org/contributions-to-chi](https://chi2026.acm.org/contributions-to-chi/). Lists eight types, including "Understanding Users", "Systems, Tools, Architectures and Infrastructure" and "Methodology". For Understanding Users it asks: "Do you clearly and transparently describe the methodology and mechanics behind the analysis…?" and "Do you identify your positionality relative to the research…?" For Systems it says validation "could be achieved by sound argumentation, reflection of experiences, user studies, or other evidence". It sets no numeric sample-size criterion. *Implication:* a positionality statement and a transparent analysis description are explicit review criteria at CHI. Include them, and IMWUT reviewers drawn from the same community will expect them too.
- **[Current] CHI 2025. "Guide to Reviewing Papers."** [chi2025.acm.org/guide-to-reviewing-papers](https://chi2025.acm.org/guide-to-reviewing-papers/). Tells reviewers that "a paper can make a contribution to HCI in many ways, and you should review the paper appropriately". The page I retrieved gives no specific guidance on small sample sizes. *Implication:* the paper must state its contribution type early so reviewers judge it against the right yardstick.
- **[Current] UbiComp/ISWC 2024. "IMWUT papers" call.** [ubicomp.org/ubicomp-iswc-2024/imwut_papers](https://ubicomp.org/ubicomp-iswc-2024/imwut_papers/). IMWUT has three submission deadlines per year: 1 February, 1 May and 1 November. Detailed reviewing information is on the ACM DL IMWUT pages, which returned 403 when I tried to fetch them. *Implication:* 1 Nov 2026 is a regular IMWUT cycle. IMWUT's revise-and-resubmit model allows a later round to add interview data if reviewers ask for it.

**Small-n deployment logics (probes, formative evaluation, RtD)**
- **[Foundational] Hutchinson, H., Mackay, W., Westerlund, B., Bederson, B.B., Druin, A., Plaisant, C., et al. (2003). "Technology probes: inspiring design for and with families." *CHI 2003*.** [doi:10.1145/642611.642616](https://doi.org/10.1145/642611.642616); [PDF](https://www.cs.ucf.edu/~lboloni/Teaching/EEL6788_2010/papers/Hutchinson-TechnologyProbes.pdf). Probes are "simple, flexible, adaptable technologies with three interdisciplinary goals": a social-science goal (understand needs in a real-world setting), an engineering goal (field-test the technology) and a design goal (inspire users and researchers). Unlike prototypes, probes "are not changed during the use period based on user feedback." Their logging "allows researchers to create visualizations of the use of the probes, which can be discussed by both users and designers." *Implication:* the probe framing fits the "formative" aim and directly licenses log-visualisation-prompted interviews. If Souffleur was updated during the deployment, call it a research prototype or iterative deployment rather than a pure probe.
- **[Foundational] Klasnja, P., Consolvo, S. & Pratt, W. (2011). "How to evaluate technologies for health behavior change in HCI research." *CHI 2011*.** [doi:10.1145/1978942.1979396](https://doi.org/10.1145/1978942.1979396). Argues that demonstrating behaviour change "is often infeasible and unnecessary for a meaningful contribution to HCI research, especially when in the early stages of design or when evaluating novel technologies". HCI work should instead evaluate the specific strategies the system embodies and seek "a deep understanding of people's experiences with the technology" (summary via [search abstract](https://www.researchgate.net/profile/Wanda-Pratt/publication/221516657_How_to_evaluate_technologies_for_health_behavior_change_in_HCI_research/links/00b4952603e799f72c000000/How-to-evaluate-technologies-for-health-behavior-change-in-HCI-research.pdf)). *Implication:* the closest canonical argument for not claiming that Souffleur "improves offloading or productivity". Claim an understanding of the experience and of how specific design strategies work (spoken debrief, drafts, proactivity).
- **[Foundational] Zimmerman, J., Forlizzi, J. & Evenson, S. (2007). "Research through design as a method for interaction design research in HCI." *CHI 2007*.** [doi:10.1145/1240624.1240704](https://doi.org/10.1145/1240624.1240704). The canonical argument that designed artifacts and the process of making them are legitimate knowledge contributions, judged by process, invention, relevance and extensibility (from my knowledge of the paper). *Implication:* an RtD framing lets the pilot write-ups and design rationale count as data, not just as preamble.
- **[Foundational] Gaver, W. (2012). "What should we expect from research through design?" *CHI 2012*.** [doi:10.1145/2207676.2208538](https://doi.org/10.1145/2207676.2208538). Argues that RtD knowledge is provisional, contingent and "annotative" rather than convergent standards. Do not hold RtD to replicability norms (from my knowledge of the paper). *Implication:* supports presenting design implications as situated and generative, not as generalisable guidelines.
- **[Foundational] Odom, W., Wakkary, R., Lim, Y., Desjardins, A., Hengeveld, B. & Banks, R. (2016). "From research prototype to research product." *CHI 2016*.** [doi:10.1145/2858036.2858447](https://doi.org/10.1145/2858036.2858447). Proposes "research products": artifacts finished enough to live with independently in the field, characterised by inquiry-driven purpose, finish, fit and independence (from my knowledge; no abstract was available via API). *Implication:* Souffleur used unsupervised in real commutes fits the "research product" logic. This helps argue why a finished-enough system deployed in the wild yields knowledge that a lab session cannot.
- **[Foundational] Kjeldskov, J. & Skov, M.B. (2014). "Was it worth the hassle? Ten years of mobile HCI research discussions on lab and field evaluations." *MobileHCI 2014*.** [doi:10.1145/2628363.2628398](https://doi.org/10.1145/2628363.2628398); [PDF](https://vbn.aau.dk/ws/files/201676914/Kjeldskov_C80.pdf). Reviewing ten years of literature, they find "no definite answer to the lab versus field question" and conclude "the real question is not if – but when and how – to go into the field". They urge moving "beyond usability evaluations" toward studies that are "truly in-the-wild, and longitudinal". They also suggest "sometimes sacrificing some of the direct researcher involvement" to extend time in use. *Implication:* the commute context is unsimulable, and there is no researcher in the car. Frame both as the point of the study, not as a weakness.
- **[Foundational] Rogers, Y., Connelly, K., Tedesco, L., Hazlewood, W., Kurtz, A., Hall, R.E., Hursey, J. & Toscos, T. (2007). "Why it's worth the hassle: the value of in-situ studies when designing ubicomp." *UbiComp 2007*, LNCS 4717.** [doi:10.1007/978-3-540-74853-3_20](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-540-74853-3_20). Widely cited argument that in-situ deployments reveal use patterns lab studies miss. I verified only metadata, not the full text. *Implication:* a standard citation for the in-situ rationale.
- **[Foundational] Rogers, Y. & Marshall, P. (2017). *Research in the Wild*. Synthesis Lectures on Human-Centered Informatics.** [doi:10.1007/978-3-031-02220-3](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-031-02220-3). Book-length treatment of "in the wild" research. Kjærup et al. quote from it that "papers must be written, and research budgets are tight" to explain the tension between duration and cost. *Implication:* useful to cite when justifying a 4-week window.

**Sample-size and duration norms**
- **[Foundational] Caine, K. (2016). "Local standards for sample size at CHI." *CHI 2016*.** [doi:10.1145/2858036.2858498](https://doi.org/10.1145/2858036.2858498); [abstract](https://open.clemson.edu/computing_pubs/28). Analysing all CHI 2014 papers, sample sizes ranged "from 1 to 916,000 and the most common sample size is 12". Sample size "differs based on factors such as study setting and type of methodology employed". *Implication:* cite as the local standard. N≈6 sits below the mode, so justify it by information power and depth rather than by norm.
- **[Foundational] Kjærup, M., Skov, M.B., Nielsen, P.A., Kjeldskov, J., Gerken, J. & Reiterer, H. (2021). "Longitudinal studies in HCI research: a review of CHI publications from 1982–2019." In *Advances in Longitudinal HCI Research* (Springer HCI Series).** [doi:10.1007/978-3-030-67322-2_2](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-030-67322-2_2); [author PDF](https://vbn.aau.dk/ws/files/414964186/Review_Longitudinal_HCI_chapter_Author_version.pdf). Of 106 longitudinal CHI papers: 16 lasted ≤14 days, 12 lasted 2–4 weeks, 31 lasted 1–11 months, 25 lasted ≥1 year and 22 did not specify. For "understanding users" papers, 30 of 43 (71%) ran at least one month. Interface papers were mostly under a month (85%). Quote: "When novelty bias wears off, the integration into routines and habits begin… How long this takes depends on the cycles inherent in the object and context of the study." *Implication:* about 4 weeks is legitimate, but the paper should call itself "formative / early-adoption" rather than "longitudinal". A commute cycle is daily or weekly, so 4 weeks gives the heavy user many cycles and the light users few.
- **[Foundational] Malterud, K., Siersma, V.D. & Guassora, A.D. (2016). "Sample size in qualitative interview studies: guided by information power." *Qualitative Health Research* 26(13).** [doi:10.1177/1049732315617444](https://doi.org/10.1177/1049732315617444) (summary via [CASRAI](https://www.casrai.org/guides/data-saturation-information-power-qualitative-sample-size)). The more information relevant to the study a sample holds, the fewer participants are needed. Information power depends on (a) study aim (narrow vs broad), (b) sample specificity, (c) use of established theory, (d) quality of dialogue and (e) analysis strategy (case-oriented vs cross-case). *Implication:* the best single citation for justifying N≈6. A narrow aim (voice offloading on commutes), a highly specific sample (real commuters who used it), theory-informed analysis (e.g., cognitive offloading) and case-oriented analysis all raise information power.
- **[Current] Hennink, M. & Kaiser, B.N. (2022). "Sample sizes for saturation in qualitative research: a systematic review of empirical tests." *Social Science & Medicine* 292.** [doi:10.1016/j.socscimed.2021.114523](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.socscimed.2021.114523). Across 23 empirical or modelling studies, saturation was reached at "9–17" interviews or "4–8" focus groups, "particularly those with relatively homogenous study populations and narrowly defined objectives". *Implication:* if a reviewer invokes saturation, N≈6 is below this band. Better to avoid saturation claims (see Braun & Clarke 2019 in Section 4) and argue from information power and per-case depth.

### Inferences
- **The strongest framing given the data** is "a formative, in-the-wild deployment study (empirical study of system use) of a voice thinking partner during commutes". It should contribute (i) empirically grounded accounts of how people use, or do not use, spoken offloading while driving, (ii) design considerations or tensions, and (iii) a secondary methodological contribution: a privacy-preserving study design for intimate voice AI. Klasnja et al. license dropping efficacy claims. Hutchinson et al. license log-visualisation interviews. Malterud et al. license N≈6.
- **"Probe" vs "prototype".** Hutchinson et al. say probes are not changed during use. If Souffleur shipped features mid-study (the repo's recent commits suggest active development, e.g., draft-card versioning and status pills), call it a "research prototype" and report a change log aligned to the deployment timeline. Koch et al. 2018 (Section 6) show that changes cause recurring novelty spikes.
- **The onboarding framing fits the data distribution.** Most participants made 1–9 drives, so the corpus is richest about first encounters, adoption barriers and early abandonment. An "early adoption / first weeks with a voice thinking partner" framing matches what was actually observed better than claims about sustained offloading. The heavy user's 36 drives can then serve as an "information-rich case" of what sustained use can look like (see Section 2).
- **IMWUT vs CHI.** IMWUT papers often pair a deployment with a technical system contribution (e.g., MindScape's sensing plus LLM pipeline). If the system has a distinctive technical element (on-device privacy logging, the debrief pipeline, proactive timing), foregrounding it strengthens the IMWUT fit. Otherwise CHI/CSCW/DIS may judge an "understanding users" paper on more favourable terms.

### Gaps
- I found no systematic census of sample sizes or durations specifically for IMWUT 2022–2026 deployment papers. The figures above are drawn from individual precedents, not a representative sample.
- IMWUT's own reviewer criteria page (dl.acm.org/journal/imwut and imwut.acm.org) returned HTTP 403, so I could not quote IMWUT-specific criteria.
- The full text of Rogers et al. 2007 was not retrieved. Its content is cited only by title and reputation.

## 2. Mixed-methods designs (logs + micro-surveys + diaries + interviews), and handling sparse self-report and uneven participation

### Takeaway
The standard ubicomp recipe triangulates passive logs, in-situ self-report (ESM or diaries) and post-study interviews. Its most powerful move for small n is the data-prompted (log-informed) interview, where participants interpret visualisations of their own usage. Spoken debriefs map onto the established voice-mail diary method. Uneven participation should be made visible and analysed, not averaged away. Field-trial methodologists explicitly warn that results often rest on a subset of participants. Per-participant engagement tables, case-based analysis (heavy user as information-rich case), and treating non-use as data are the accepted strategies.

### Cited Findings

**Combining data streams**
- **[Foundational] Consolvo, S., Harrison, B., Smith, I., Chen, M.Y., Everitt, K., Froehlich, J. & Landay, J.A. (2007). "Conducting in situ evaluations for and with ubiquitous computing technologies." *IJHCI* 22(1–2).** [doi:10.1080/10447310709336957](https://doi.org/10.1080/10447310709336957). A methods overview of in-situ ubicomp evaluation that combines experience sampling, logging, diaries and interviews (described from my knowledge; only metadata was verified). *Implication:* the canonical citation for the logs + self-report + interview triangulation.
- **[Foundational] van Berkel, N., Ferreira, D. & Kostakos, V. (2017). "The experience sampling method on mobile devices." *ACM Computing Surveys* 50(6).** [doi:10.1145/3123988](https://doi.org/10.1145/3123988). Survey of mobile ESM design choices: triggers, schedules, compliance and participant burden (described from my knowledge; abstract not retrieved). *Implication:* sparse 1–7 ratings (6 from 5 people) are a known ESM compliance failure mode. Report the response rate per participant and treat the ratings as illustrative, not as an outcome measure.
- **[Foundational] Palen, L. & Salzman, M. (2002). "Voice-mail diary studies for naturalistic data capture under mobile conditions." *CSCW 2002*.** [doi:10.1145/587078.587092](https://doi.org/10.1145/587078.587092); [PDF](https://cmci.colorado.edu/~palen/palen_papers/palen-diarystudy.pdf). Extends paper diaries by "using voice-mail paired with mobile and landline telephony to more easily collect data in natural situations". It discusses data quality and quantity, "the degree and nature of subject participation", compensation schedules and transcription. *Implication:* the direct precedent for Souffleur's spoken post-drive debrief. Cite it to frame the about 23 debriefs as voice-diary entries and to justify their format, which suits a mobile context where hands are busy.
- **[Foundational] Carter, S. & Mankoff, J. (2005). "When participants do the capturing: the role of media in diary studies." *CHI 2005*.** [doi:10.1145/1054972.1055098](https://doi.org/10.1145/1054972.1055098). Compares capture media (photos, audio, tangible objects) for diary studies and their use as prompts in later interviews (described from my knowledge; abstract elided by publisher). *Implication:* supports using participant-captured items (shareable drafts) as interview prompts.
- **[Foundational] Pradhan, A., Findlater, L. & Lazar, A. (2019). "'Phantom Friend' or 'Just a Box with Information': personification and ontological categorization of smart speaker-based voice assistants by older adults." *PACM HCI (CSCW)* 3, Art. 214.** [doi:10.1145/3359316](https://doi.org/10.1145/3359316); [PDF](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10157641). Deployed Echo Dots with 7 older adults for 3 weeks. Data came from "weekly in-person interviews and daily diary check-ins via automated phone calls, and collected usage logs". The authors frame their work as "a preliminary assessment". *Implication:* a near-identical mixed-methods template (logs + phone-based voice diaries + interviews, N=7, 3 weeks) published at CSCW, with modest "preliminary" framing.
- **[Foundational] Ammari, T., Kaye, J., Tsai, J.Y. & Bentley, F. (2019). "Music, search, and IoT: how people (really) use voice assistants." *ACM TOCHI* 26(3).** [doi:10.1145/3311956](https://doi.org/10.1145/3311956). Combined interviews with 19 users and log files from 82 Alexa devices (193,665 commands) and 88 Google Home devices (65,499 commands). *Implication:* shows the log-plus-interview combination for voice assistants. Souffleur's logs are counts and timings only, so the qualitative side must carry more of the interpretive weight.

**Log-informed, data-prompted and elicitation interviews**
- **[Foundational] Kwasnicka, D., Dombrowski, S.U., White, M. & Sniehotta, F.F. (2015). "Data-prompted interviews: using individual ecological data to stimulate narratives and explore meanings." *Health Psychology* 34(12).** [doi:10.1037/hea0000234](https://doi.org/10.1037/hea0000234); [PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4671473/). Data-prompted interviews (DPIs) use personal data collected beforehand (graphs, audio, text, EMA) to "stimulate visual and auditory senses, enhance memory, and prompt rich narratives anchored in personal experiences". They let researchers "explore the meaning of the data and … explain data patterns". The main challenges are data selection and presentation. *Implication:* the citable name for the planned final interviews. Show each participant their own drive timeline (counts, durations, debrief dates) to prompt narrative, without anyone reading content.
- **[Foundational] Hogan, T., Hinrichs, U. & Hornecker, E. (2016). "The Elicitation Interview technique: capturing people's experiences of data representations." *IEEE TVCG* 22(12).** [doi:10.1109/TVCG.2015.2511718](https://doi.org/10.1109/TVCG.2015.2511718). Adapts the elicitation interview (from psycho-phenomenology) to obtain "detailed and precise accounts of human experience" of a specific moment with data. It reveals interpretation "and stories beyond the represented data" (via [St Andrews summary](https://research-portal.st-andrews.ac.uk/en/publications/the-elicitation-interview-technique-capturing-peoples-experiences/)). *Implication:* use elicitation-style questioning ("take me back to that drive on Tuesday…") anchored on a single logged drive to get fine-grained experiential detail that sparse ratings cannot.
- **[Current] Griffioen, N., van Rooij, M., Lichtwarck-Aschoff, A. & Granic, I. (2020). "A stimulated recall method for the improved assessment of quantity and quality of social media use." *JMIR* 22(1).** [doi:10.2196/15529](https://doi.org/10.2196/15529). Researcher and participant jointly reviewed screen video and in-phone logs "in an act of co-research", discussing why, what, with whom and how they felt. Participants rated the method pleasant (7.46/10). Limitations: the setup is lab-dependent, "privacy issues would arise" in the field, and it does not suit multi-day behaviour. *Implication:* supports stimulated recall with logs. It also shows why Souffleur cannot replay content: the privacy promise forbids it. The workaround is to replay metadata and participant-shared items only.
- **[Current] Bruun, A., Law, E.L.-C., Heintz, M. & Eriksen, P.S. (2016). "Asserting real-time emotions through cued-recall." *NordiCHI 2016*.** [doi:10.1145/2971485.2971516](https://doi.org/10.1145/2971485.2971516). Validates cued recall for UX evaluation as an alternative that reduces memory bias in emotion measurement (per the search summary). *Implication:* cued recall from timestamps can partly substitute for missing in-the-moment ratings.
- **[Foundational] Flanagan, J.C. (1954). "The critical incident technique." *Psychological Bulletin* 51(4).** [doi:10.1037/h0061470](https://doi.org/10.1037/h0061470). The classic method for eliciting specific, behaviourally described incidents of notable success or failure. *Implication:* structure interview and debrief prompts around "the drive where it most helped / most got in the way". This yields concrete, analysable episodes from light users with few drives.
- **[Foundational] Hartson, H.R. & Castillo, J.C. (1998). "Remote evaluation for post-deployment usability improvement." *AVI 1998*.** [doi:10.1145/948496.948499](https://doi.org/10.1145/948496.948499). User-reported critical incidents captured remotely after deployment (from my knowledge; metadata verified). *Implication:* a precedent for participant-initiated incident reporting. A "flag this moment" voice command or a shareable mark is a CIT-like instrument.

**Sparse self-report, uneven participation, non-use**
- **[Foundational] Brown, B., Reeves, S. & Sherwood, S. (2011). "Into the wild: challenges and opportunities for field trial methods." *CHI 2011*.** [doi:10.1145/1978942.1979185](https://doi.org/10.1145/1978942.1979185); [abstract](https://eprints.nottingham.ac.uk/1823). Documents "demand characteristics" (users adjust behaviour to trial expectations), "the interdependence of how trials are run and the result they produce", and "how trial results can be dependent on the insights of a subset of trial participants". It offers three strategies to leverage these challenges. *Implication:* name the heavy-user skew openly. It is a recognised property of field trials, and hiding it is worse than analysing it.
- **[Foundational] Eysenbach, G. (2005). "The law of attrition." *JMIR* 7(1).** [doi:10.2196/jmir.7.1.e11](https://doi.org/10.2196/jmir.7.1.e11). "In any eHealth trial a substantial proportion of users drop out before completion or stop using the application". Researchers "tend to gloss over high dropout rates". Eysenbach calls for a "science of attrition". *Implication:* report the 1–9-drive users' trajectories as findings about attrition and adoption, not as missing data.
- **[Foundational] Satchell, C. & Dourish, P. (2009). "Beyond the user: use and non-use in HCI." *OZCHI 2009*.** [doi:10.1145/1738826.1738829](https://doi.org/10.1145/1738826.1738829). Argues that non-use (lagging adoption, active resistance, disenchantment) is analytically meaningful, not merely absence (from my knowledge; metadata verified). *Implication:* gives a conceptual vocabulary for light users' limited engagement.
- **[Foundational] Baumer, E.P.S., Adams, P., Khovanskaya, V.D., Liao, T.C., Smith, M.E., Schwanda Sosik, V. & Williams, K. (2013). "Limiting, leaving, and (re)lapsing: an exploration of Facebook non-use practices." *CHI 2013*.** [doi:10.1145/2470654.2466446](https://doi.org/10.1145/2470654.2466446); and **Epstein, D.A., Caraway, M., Johnston, C., Ping, A., Fogarty, J. & Munson, S.A. (2016). "Beyond abandonment to next steps: understanding and designing for life after personal informatics tool use." *CHI 2016*.** [doi:10.1145/2858036.2858045](https://doi.org/10.1145/2858036.2858045). Both treat reduced use, lapsing and abandonment as varied practices worth designing for (from my knowledge; metadata verified). *Implication:* interview light users specifically about why they did not drive with it, or stopped. That is first-class data for an onboarding framing.
- **[Current] Bowman, R., Nadal, C., Morrissey, K., Thieme, A. & Doherty, G. (2023). "Using thematic analysis in healthcare HCI at CHI: a scoping review." *CHI 2023*.** [doi:10.1145/3544548.3581203](https://doi.org/10.1145/3544548.3581203); [PDF](https://microsoft.com/en-us/research/uploads/prod/2023/09/Thematic_Analysis_2023.pdf). Notes "interesting examples of reporting included a participant incidence matrix" and summary tables. 28 of 100 TAs reported participant counts per theme. *Implication:* a participant × theme (or participant × data-source) incidence matrix is a recognised way to show readers whether a theme rests on the heavy user alone.
- **[Foundational] Dragicevic, P. (2016). "Fair statistical communication in HCI." In *Modern Statistical Methods for HCI* (Springer HCI Series).** [doi:10.1007/978-3-319-26633-6_13](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-319-26633-6_13). Argues for estimation and transparent reporting over dichotomous significance testing (from my knowledge; metadata verified). *Implication:* with 6 ratings, show all raw values (e.g., a dot plot by participant), not means or tests.
- **[Current] Kraus, M., Zepf, S., Westhäußer, R., Feustel, I., Zargham, N., Aslan, I., Edwards, J., Mayer, S., Kontogiorgos, D., Wagner, N. & André, E. (2025). "BEHAVE AI: best practices and guidelines for human-centric design and evaluation of proactive AI agents." *IUI Companion 2025* (workshop).** [doi:10.1145/3708557.3716155](https://doi.org/10.1145/3708557.3716155). Notes that standard instruments such as SUS and UEQ "refer to reactive GUIs and may, therefore, be of limited applicability" to proactive agents. *Implication:* supports not leaning on standard questionnaires for a proactive voice partner, and motivates bespoke debrief and interview instruments.

### Inferences
- **Suggested analysis plan that fits the data actually collected:**
  1. **Engagement table and timeline per participant.** Columns: drives, total talk minutes, active days, debriefs completed, ratings given, items shared, PostHog onboarding steps reached, account type. Exclude or separately label guest, test and researcher accounts. This is the "incidence matrix" logic of Bowman et al.
  2. **Debriefs as voice-diary entries** (Palen & Salzman). Code them reflexively alongside interview transcripts. Report how many debriefs each theme draws on.
  3. **Data-prompted final interviews** (Kwasnicka et al.; Hutchinson et al.'s log visualisations). Show each participant only their own metadata timeline plus whatever they marked shareable. Use critical-incident prompts (Flanagan) and elicitation-style probing of one or two specific drives (Hogan et al.).
  4. **Case-based reporting.** A detailed vignette of the heavy user as an "information-rich case" of sustained use. A cross-case synthesis of light users, focused on onboarding, barriers and non-use (Satchell & Dourish; Epstein et al.). The two pilot sessions can be a "formative Study 1" that motivated design decisions.
  5. **Ratings and PostHog as descriptive context only.** Report raw ratings. Use PostHog funnels to locate onboarding drop-off points, which then become interview probes.
- **Uneven participation is the main threat and the main finding opportunity.** Brown et al. warn that results can hinge on a subset of participants. Stating "most themes about sustained use derive from P_x; themes about adoption derive from all" is honest and expected by reviewers.

### Gaps
- I did not find an HCI methods paper that specifically prescribes "idiographic" or N-of-1 analysis for deployment studies. The case-study recommendation rests on Malterud et al.'s "case-oriented analysis" dimension, Brown et al. and general qualitative practice. A dedicated HCI citation (e.g., from first-person or case-study methods) would strengthen it.
- I found no ESM or post-drive micro-survey method paper specific to in-car use.

## 3. Privacy-preserving research designs where researchers cannot read content

### Takeaway
There is solid precedent for (a) content-free or abstracted logging justified by privacy (LanguageLogger, ResearchIME), (b) participant-controlled data donation, where participants review and filter before sharing, (c) participants interpreting their own intimate data in interviews, and (d) aggregate, machine-only analysis of AI conversations. Reviewers accept these designs when the paper (i) states the trade-off explicitly, (ii) explains what the design makes possible ethically (studying intimate, everyday use), and (iii) shows how qualitative channels compensate for the lack of content. Contrast cases that did record audio make the trade-off concrete.

### Cited Findings
- **[Foundational] Bemmann, F. & Buschek, D. (2020). "LanguageLogger: a mobile keyboard application for studying language use in everyday text communication in the wild." *PACM HCI (EICS)* 4.** [doi:10.1145/3397872](https://doi.org/10.1145/3397872). Collects language-use data "without logging readable messages to preserve privacy", using three on-device text abstraction methods (word categorisation, regex filtering, whitelist counting). The authors simulated text-reconstruction attacks to set safe parameters and ran "a two-week field deployment (N=20)" assessing participants' experiences. *Implication:* the closest precedent for counts-only logging justified to reviewers. Borrow its moves: specify exactly what is logged, show it cannot reconstruct content, and evaluate participants' trust in the design.
- **[Foundational] Buschek, D., Bisinger, B. & Alt, F. (2018). "ResearchIME: a mobile keyboard application for studying free typing behaviour in the wild." *CHI 2018*.** [doi:10.1145/3173574.3173829](https://doi.org/10.1145/3173574.3173829). A research keyboard for in-the-wild typing studies, the predecessor to LanguageLogger. I verified the metadata only. *Implication:* evidence that CHI accepts instrumented, privacy-aware logging as a method contribution.
- **[Current] Boeschoten, L., Ausloos, J., Möller, J.E., Araujo, T. & Oberski, D.L. (2022). "A framework for privacy preserving digital trace data collection through data donation." *Computational Communication Research* 4(2).** [doi:10.5117/CCR2022.2.002.BOES](https://doi.org/10.5117/CCR2022.2.002.BOES); [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2011.09851). Participants process their data locally to "extract only the data points relevant", review the extracted data, and only then consent to share. The paper adds a "total error framework" for such studies. *Implication:* Souffleur's "mark as shareable" flow is a form of participant-filtered data donation. Cite this framework and discuss its selection-bias errors: people share their best moments.
- **[Current] Gomez Ortega, A., Bourgeois, J. & Kortuem, G. (2022). "Reconstructing intimate contexts through data donation: a case study in menstrual tracking technologies." *NordiCHI 2022*.** [doi:10.1145/3546155.3546646](https://doi.org/10.1145/3546155.3546646). Extends data donation "beyond data collection to … actively engaging data donors in the (intimate) interpretation of their data". 35 donors gave data over five weeks, and 13 of them took part in reconstructing its context. *Implication:* a direct precedent for having participants, not researchers, interpret private data. It maps onto final interviews where participants choose what to show.
- **[Current] Gomez Ortega, A., Bourgeois, J., Hutiri, W.T. & Kortuem, G. (2023). "Beyond data transactions: a framework for meaningfully informed data donation." *AI & Society*.** [doi:10.1007/s00146-023-01755-5](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00146-023-01755-5). A conceptual framework for donation that is meaningfully informed rather than transactional (metadata verified; content summarised from title and abstract-level knowledge). *Implication:* useful for the ethics and consent section.
- **[Current] Razi, A., Alsoubai, A., Kim, S., Naher, N., Ali, S., Stringhini, G., De Choudhury, M. & Wisniewski, P. (2022). "Instagram data donation: a case study on collecting ecologically valid social media data for the purpose of adolescent online risk detection." *CHI EA 2022*.** [doi:10.1145/3491101.3503569](https://doi.org/10.1145/3491101.3503569). Youth "donate and annotate their Instagram data", with participants labelling their own private conversations. *Implication:* a precedent for participants annotating their own private content. Souffleur could ask participants to self-categorise drives (topic type, usefulness) without disclosing content.
- **[Current] Ammari, T., Chen, M., Zaman, S.M.M. & Garimella, K. (2025). "How students (really) use ChatGPT: uncovering experiences among undergraduate students." *arXiv:2505.24126*.** [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.24126). Analysed "10,536 naturalistic messages donated by 36 students over a year". Logs were exported by participants and de-identified, then coded using LLM annotation validated against human labels (κ=0.75–0.91). *Implication:* shows the norm in LLM-use research is donated full logs. Souffleur's stricter no-read design is unusual and can be framed as a deliberate ethical choice for an intimate "thinking out loud" context.
- **[Current] Tamkin, A., McCain, M., Handa, K., Durmus, E., Lovitt, L., Rathi, A., et al. (2024). "Clio: privacy-preserving insights into real-world AI use." *arXiv:2412.13678*.** [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.13678). Uses AI models to produce aggregated usage patterns "without the need for human reviewers to read raw conversations". It reports 94% accuracy against synthetic ground truth and no private-data clusters in a 5,000-conversation audit (per search summary). *Implication:* a possible route to content-level aggregate insight, but only if participants' consent explicitly covers automated analysis. The current promise ("nobody reads or listens") arguably does not cover it, so do not apply it retroactively without re-consent.
- **[Foundational] Sciuto, A., Saini, A., Forlizzi, J. & Hong, J.I. (2018). "'Hey Alexa, what's up?': a mixed-methods study of in-home conversational agent usage." *DIS 2018*.** [doi:10.1145/3196709.3196772](https://doi.org/10.1145/3196709.3196772). Gathered "history logs of 75 Alexa participants" (over 278,000 commands) plus seven in-home contextual interviews. *Implication:* a precedent for participant-donated voice-assistant histories. Souffleur intentionally does not do this, and should say why: the content is personal thinking, not commands.
- **[Foundational] Light, B., Burgess, J. & Duguay, S. (2018). "The walkthrough method: an approach to the study of apps." *New Media & Society* 20(3).** [doi:10.1177/1461444816675438](https://doi.org/10.1177/1461444816675438). A structured method for examining an app's "environment of expected use" and technical walkthrough (from my knowledge; metadata verified). Duguay & Gold-Apel (2023, [doi:10.1177/20563051231158822](https://doi.org/10.1177/20563051231158822)) revisit its limits. *Implication:* a participant-led walkthrough (participants showing their own app history on their own screen and choosing what to reveal) is an adaptation of this method. Cite it as inspiration and describe the adaptation explicitly.
- **Contrast cases (content recorded):**
  - **[Foundational] Porcheron, M., Fischer, J.E., Reeves, S. & Sharples, S. (2018). "Voice interfaces in everyday life." *CHI 2018*.** [doi:10.1145/3173574.3174214](https://doi.org/10.1145/3173574.3174214). Collected "audio data from month-long deployments of the Amazon Echo" for conversation analysis.
  - **[Current] Mahmood, A., Wang, J. & Huang, C.-M. (2026). "Situated understanding of errors in older adults' interactions with voice assistants: a month-long, in-home study." *ACM TACCESS* 19(1).** [doi:10.1145/3796236](https://doi.org/10.1145/3796236). Argues that "usage logs and post hoc interviews… do not fully capture the intricacies" of error interactions, so it equipped 15 homes with custom audio recorders.
  - *Implication:* reviewers familiar with these studies will ask what is lost without content. Pre-empt by stating which research questions the design cannot answer (e.g., fine-grained breakdown analysis) and which it is better suited to (willingness to think aloud privately, trust, felt privacy).

### Inferences
- **Recommended justification pattern for reviewers:**
  1. The study context is intimate: unfiltered spoken thinking, possibly about work, family and health. Recording or reading it would change the behaviour under study (cf. Brown et al.'s demand characteristics) and would deter recruitment.
  2. Specify the logging schema precisely, as LanguageLogger does: what is counted and timed, and what is never stored or accessible.
  3. Describe three participant-controlled channels: debrief, shareable marking, interview show-and-tell. Frame them as participant-filtered data donation (Boeschoten et al.) with participant interpretation (Gomez Ortega et al.).
  4. Acknowledge the selection bias of shared items.
  5. Optionally, report participants' views of the privacy design as a finding in its own right.
- This privacy design could be a defensible secondary methodological contribution, "studying intimate voice-AI use without reading it". It needs reflection on what worked: e.g., the debrief completion rate (about 23 debriefs against total drives) and how much was shared.

### Gaps
- I found no published HCI study of a private journaling or voice-diary app in which researchers had no content access at all and relied only on participant-selected excerpts plus counts. The closest are LanguageLogger (abstracted content) and data-donation studies (participant-filtered content). This absence may itself support a novelty claim, but I could not confirm it exhaustively.
- I found no reviewer-facing guidance (e.g., from IMWUT or CHI) on what counts as acceptable evidence when content is inaccessible.

## 4. Qualitative analysis approaches and how to report them

### Takeaway
Reflexive thematic analysis (rTA) is the dominant and well-understood choice in HCI. Its main reporting failures are well documented: mixing rTA with inter-rater reliability, "themes emerged" language, saturation claims, no positionality, and thin descriptions of theme generation. Reviewers increasingly accept the argument that qualitative work should be judged on interpretive criteria (reflexivity, grounded and evident findings, analytic reach, relevance), not on sample size or IRR. Affinity diagramming is an accepted, design-oriented alternative for prototype evaluation data.

### Cited Findings
- **[Foundational] Braun, V. & Clarke, V. (2006). "Using thematic analysis in psychology." *Qualitative Research in Psychology* 3(2).** [doi:10.1191/1478088706qp063oa](https://doi.org/10.1191/1478088706qp063oa). The original six-phase TA guide. *Implication:* still the default citation, but pair it with the 2019 and 2021 updates to signal reflexive TA.
- **[Foundational] Braun, V. & Clarke, V. (2019). "Reflecting on reflexive thematic analysis." *Qualitative Research in Sport, Exercise and Health* 11(4).** [doi:10.1080/2159676X.2019.1628806](https://doi.org/10.1080/2159676X.2019.1628806). Revisits the 2006 paper's "unspoken assumptions" and clarifies "a method we now prefer to call reflexive TA". *Implication:* cite it to name the specific variant used.
- **[Current] Braun, V. & Clarke, V. (2021; online 2020). "One size fits all? What counts as quality practice in (reflexive) thematic analysis?" *Qualitative Research in Psychology* 18(3).** [doi:10.1080/14780887.2020.1769238](https://doi.org/10.1080/14780887.2020.1769238). Warns that reviewers "promote 'coding reliability measures' as universal requirements". Identifies ten common problems and offers "twenty critical questions" for reviewers and editors. *Implication:* cite in the methods section to pre-empt IRR demands, and self-audit against the 20 questions.
- **[Foundational] Braun, V. & Clarke, V. (2019). "To saturate or not to saturate? Questioning data saturation as a useful concept for thematic analysis and sample-size rationales." *QRSEH* 13(2).** [doi:10.1080/2159676X.2019.1704846](https://doi.org/10.1080/2159676X.2019.1704846). Argues that saturation, understood as "information redundancy", is coherent with neo-positivist TA but not with reflexive TA. *Implication:* do not claim saturation with N≈6. Justify the sample by information power instead.
- **[Current] Braun, V. & Clarke, V. (2023; online 2022). "Toward good practice in thematic analysis: avoiding common problems and be(com)ing a knowing researcher." *International Journal of Transgender Health* 24(1).** [doi:10.1080/26895269.2022.2129597](https://doi.org/10.1080/26895269.2022.2129597). Practical guide to common TA problems (metadata verified; content per title). *Implication:* a current citation for "knowing" methodological choices.
- **[Foundational] McDonald, N., Schoenebeck, S. & Forte, A. (2019). "Reliability and inter-rater reliability in qualitative research: norms and guidelines for CSCW and HCI practice." *PACM HCI (CSCW)* 3, Art. 72.** [doi:10.1145/3359174](https://doi.org/10.1145/3359174). A meta-analysis of CSCW and HCI papers from 2016–2018 finds "IRR is rare, occurring in around 1/9 of qualitative papers". It proposes guidelines on when IRR is and is not appropriate. *Implication:* with a single analyst or interpretive aims, cite McDonald et al. to explain why no κ is reported. Describe instead how the team discussed and challenged interpretations.
- **[Current] Bowman, R., Nadal, C., Morrissey, K., Thieme, A. & Doherty, G. (2023). "Using thematic analysis in healthcare HCI at CHI: a scoping review." *CHI 2023*.** [doi:10.1145/3544548.3581203](https://doi.org/10.1145/3544548.3581203). The review covered 78 papers containing 100 TAs.
  - Reflexive TA was "cited in 59% of papers, yet we found little reporting of researchers' positions or reflexive practices".
  - "40% of the TAs… did not provide any description of the theme generation". A further 11 said only that themes "emerged".
  - 20% "did not explicitly report themes".
  - Of 51 TAs citing Braun & Clarke, a third reported non-reflexive practices. Grounded-theory techniques such as axial coding often appeared in TAs citing reflexive TA.
  - The authors recommend fuller reporting, for example in supplementary material.
  *Implication:* a checklist of what not to do. Include positionality, a concrete theme-generation account, and themes as patterns of shared meaning (not topic summaries). Consider an audit trail in supplementary material.
- **[Current] Crabtree, A. (2025). "H is for human and how (not) to evaluate qualitative research in HCI." *Human–Computer Interaction* (journal).** [doi:10.1080/07370024.2025.2475743](https://doi.org/10.1080/07370024.2025.2475743); [arXiv:2409.01302](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.01302). Criticises reviewers who "resort to extraneous criteria, typically metrics and measurements: sample size, code counts, interrater" reliability. It proposes five criteria:
  1. Is the methodological account adequately reflexive (the researcher's relationship to participants, how they were engaged, what data was collected, the analytic approach)?
  2. Are findings apodictic, that is, evidently supported by the data shown?
  3. Does the study elaborate or introduce a "sensitising concept"?
  4. Do results have analytic reach and utility?
  5. Is relevance to HCI stated?
  The article drew published commentaries (Bardzell, Blackwell, Dourish, Nardi, Pirolli, Rode, Tolmie) and an author response ([arXiv:2604.25312](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.25312)). *Implication:* a current, high-profile citation for pre-empting "N is too small" reviews. Organising findings around one or two sensitising concepts (e.g., "a private rehearsal space", "drive-time thinking") also matches criterion 3.
- **[Current] Soden, R., Toombs, A. & Thomas, M. (2024). "Evaluating interpretive research in HCI." *ACM Interactions* 31(1).** [doi:10.1145/3633200](https://doi.org/10.1145/3633200). Discusses reviewers' growing demands for "more rigorous methods reporting" in qualitative research and "a growing misunderstanding of interpretive research practices" (from the [search summary](https://interactions.acm.org/archive/view/january-february-2024/evaluating-interpretive-research-in-hci); full text 403). *Implication:* a second current citation for interpretive evaluation criteria.
- **[Foundational] Lucero, A. (2015). "Using affinity diagrams to evaluate interactive prototypes." *INTERACT 2015*, LNCS 9297.** [doi:10.1007/978-3-319-22668-2_19](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-319-22668-2_19). A practical account of affinity diagramming as an analysis method for prototype-evaluation data (content from my knowledge; metadata verified). Kocielnik et al. (IMWUT 2018) likewise used affinity diagramming in their formative workshops ([PDF](https://faculty.washington.edu/garyhs/docs/kocielnik-UBICOMP2018-reflection.pdf)). *Implication:* affinity diagramming is a defensible, team-based, design-oriented alternative if the goal is design implications rather than interpretive themes. Do not label it rTA.
- **[Foundational] Boehner, K., Vertesi, J., Sengers, P. & Dourish, P. (2007). "How HCI interprets the probes." *CHI 2007*.** [doi:10.1145/1240624.1240789](https://doi.org/10.1145/1240624.1240789). Shows that HCI often reinterpreted Gaver's cultural probes (Gaver, Dunne & Pacenti 1999, *Interactions*, [doi:10.1145/291224.291235](https://doi.org/10.1145/291224.291235)) as objective data-gathering instruments, losing their inspirational, subjective intent (from my knowledge). *Implication:* if Souffleur is called a "probe", be explicit about which probe tradition (Hutchinson's technology probe vs Gaver's cultural probe) and analyse accordingly.

### Inferences
- **Recommended reporting package:**
  - Name the variant: "reflexive TA (Braun & Clarke 2019, 2021)", or codebook TA if the team co-codes with a framework.
  - Add a positionality paragraph: the first author built Souffleur, and possibly knows some participants. CHI 2026 asks for this explicitly.
  - Give a concrete account of the coding and theme-development steps.
  - Show themes with participant and data-source incidence: debrief / interview / pilot; heavy vs light user.
  - Report no IRR, citing McDonald et al.
  - Make no saturation claim, citing Braun & Clarke 2019. Use information power instead (Malterud et al.).
  - Organise findings around one or two sensitising concepts (Crabtree).
  - Put the codebook or an audit trail in supplementary material (Bowman et al.).
- **Fit to data types.** Debriefs (short, in-the-moment, spoken) and interviews (retrospective, data-prompted) differ in genre. Analyse them together, but mark the source of each quote, because reviewers will ask whether insights are in-situ or retrospective.

### Gaps
- I could not retrieve full texts for Soden et al. 2024 or Lucero 2015. Their summaries rest on metadata and search summaries.

## 5. Recent (2022–2026) and anchor small-n in-the-wild deployments of voice, conversational and LLM agents, and how they framed contributions

### Takeaway
Recent CHI and IMWUT work on conversational and LLM agents routinely publishes deployments with N≈5–20 over 2–8 weeks. These papers frame contributions as "understanding how people interact" plus "design considerations or implications", sometimes with a multi-stage design process where a tiny in-home study (N=5) is the last stage. In-car LLM agent research is almost entirely simulator, test-track or single-session on-road. I found no multi-week naturalistic commute deployment of an LLM voice agent. That gap is a genuine novelty argument for Souffleur.

### Cited Findings

**Recent LLM and conversational agents in the field**
- **[Current] Abbas, A., Wohn, C., Jagtap, A., Rho, E.H., Kim, Y.-H. & Lee, S.W. (2026). "'Having lunch now': understanding how users engage with a proactive agent for daily planning and self-reflection." *CHI 2026*.** [doi:10.1145/3772318.3790957](https://doi.org/10.1145/3772318.3790957); [arXiv:2509.24073](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.24073). A "14-day longitudinal study with 12 participants" with an agent "that initiated regular check-ins". Findings: participants "accepted or negotiated suggestions, developed shared mental models, reported progress, and at times resisted or disengaged". Problems included "rigidity, premature turn-taking, and overpromising". The contribution is framed as "understanding how people interact with a proactive, coach-like agent and offers design considerations". *Implication:* the closest current template: a proactive reflective agent, N=12, 2 weeks, CHI 2026, with an "interaction patterns + design considerations" framing. Disengagement is reported as a finding.
- **[Current] Nepal, S., Pillai, A., Campbell, W., Massachi, T., Heinz, M.V., Kunwar, A., Choi, E.S., Xu, X., et al. (2024). "MindScape study: integrating LLM and behavioral sensing for personalized AI-driven journaling experiences." *PACM IMWUT* 8(4).** [doi:10.1145/3699761](https://doi.org/10.1145/3699761). An "8-week exploratory study with 20 college students". It reports pre-post changes (e.g., positive affect +7%, negative affect −11%, PHQ-4 decreasing weekly by β=−0.25). *Implication:* IMWUT accepts "exploratory" LLM journaling deployments with N=20. Note that it pairs the study with a sensing and LLM system contribution and some quantitative outcomes, which Souffleur cannot match. Lean on qualitative depth instead.
- **[Current] Mahmood, A., Wang, J. & Huang, C.-M. (2026). "Situated understanding of errors in older adults' interactions with voice assistants: a month-long, in-home study." *ACM TACCESS* 19(1).** [doi:10.1145/3796236](https://doi.org/10.1145/3796236). 15 older adults' homes over about one month, with in-the-wild audio. A ChatGPT-powered VA was deployed "midway through our study" to examine how LLM integration changes error dynamics. *Implication:* shows the mid-study system-change design, which is acceptable if made explicit. Also a contrast case on content access.
- **[Current] Mahmood, A., Cao, S., Stiber, M., Antony, V.N. & Huang, C.-M. (2025). "Voice assistants for health self-management: designing for and with older adults." *CHI 2025*.** [doi:10.1145/3706598.3713839](https://doi.org/10.1145/3706598.3713839). A "five-stage design process": in-home interviews (N=17), an LLM-powered VA prototype, co-design workshops (N=10), then usability validated "through in-home studies (N = 5)". *Implication:* CHI 2025 accepted a final field stage with N=5 when it was embedded in a multi-stage design process. Souffleur's two pilots plus the deployment could be framed similarly, as staged formative work.
- **[Current] Yang, Z., Xu, X., Yao, B., Rogers, E., Zhang, S., Intille, S., Shara, N., Gao, G.G. & Wang, D. (2024). "Talk2Care: an LLM-based voice assistant for communication between healthcare providers and older adults." *PACM IMWUT* 8(2).** [doi:10.1145/3659625](https://doi.org/10.1145/3659625). Formative interview studies with older adults (N=10) and providers (N=9) led to the LLM voice-assistant system, followed by two user studies. *Implication:* IMWUT accepts a "formative study → system → user study" structure built around an LLM voice assistant, with modest Ns per stage.
- **[Current] Liu, C., Su, M., Xiang, Y., Huang, Y., Yang, Y., Zhang, K. & Fan, M. (2025). "Toward enabling natural conversation with older adults via the design of LLM-powered voice agents that support interruptions and backchannels." *CHI 2025*.** [doi:10.1145/3706598.3714228](https://doi.org/10.1145/3706598.3714228). Older adults (N=8) took part in human–human conversation, retrospective interviews and interaction with a turn-taking LLM agent, producing design considerations (per search summary). *Implication:* N=8 with retrospective interviews is accepted for "design considerations" contributions on voice-agent conversational dynamics.
- **[Current] Park, S., Subramonyam, H. & Kulkarni, C. (2023). "Thinking assistants: LLM-based conversational assistants that help users think by asking rather than answering." *arXiv:2312.06024*.** [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.06024). A lab study (N=80) plus a field deployment (223 conversations), in which "participants responded positively to approximately 65% of the responses". The authors argue that LLM agents for cognitive enhancement "should prioritize fostering reflection". I did not confirm a peer-reviewed venue. *Implication:* conceptual neighbour for a "thinking partner". Cite as a preprint.

**Anchor (pre-2022) voice and conversational-agent field deployments**
- **[Foundational] Kocielnik, R., Xiao, L., Avrahami, D. & Hsieh, G. (2018). "Reflection Companion: a conversational system for engaging users in reflection on physical activity." *PACM IMWUT* 2(2), Art. 70.** [doi:10.1145/3214273](https://doi.org/10.1145/3214273). Formative workshops (N=12, analysed with affinity diagramming) were followed by "a 2-week field deployment with 33 active Fitbit users". "As a strong indicator of our system's value, 16 of the 33 participants elected to continue using the system for two additional weeks without compensation." The contribution is framed as "implications for the design of technology-supported dialog systems for reflection". *Implication:* an IMWUT template (formative stage → deployment → design implications). It also uses voluntary continued use as an honest value signal. Check whether any Souffleur participant kept using it after the study.
- **[Foundational] Kocielnik, R., Avrahami, D., Marlow, J., Lu, D. & Hsieh, G. (2018). "Designing for workplace reflection: a chat and voice-based conversational agent." *DIS 2018*.** [doi:10.1145/3196709.3196784](https://doi.org/10.1145/3196709.3196784). Robota combined a Slack bot and an Alexa skill in "a 3-week controlled deployment". It found that "adding dedicated mobile voice interaction separate from the already busy chat modality may further enable users to step back and reflect". *Implication:* the closest precedent for voice as a reflection channel separate from the desk. The participant count was not verified.
- **[Foundational] Williams, A.C., Kaur, H., Mark, G., Loomis Thompson, A., Iqbal, S.T. & Teevan, J. (2018). "Supporting workplace detachment and reattachment with conversational intelligence." *CHI 2018*.** [doi:10.1145/3173574.3173662](https://doi.org/10.1145/3173574.3173662). The design was validated with 108 crowdworkers, then studied "for 14 days with 34 information workers", using behavioural metrics such as after-hours email counts and productivity-app use ([ISR summary](https://isr.uci.edu/node/3549.html)). *Implication:* the closest precedent for supporting day transitions with conversational agents, which is the commute's functional role. It also shows content-free behavioural metrics used as outcomes.
- **[Foundational] Cho, M., Lee, S. & Lee, K.-P. (2019). "Once a kind friend is now a thing: understanding how conversational agents at home are forgotten." *DIS 2019*.** [doi:10.1145/3322276.3322332](https://doi.org/10.1145/3322276.3322332). A 12-week study of 8 first-time-user households using diaries, surveys and interviews, showing "how conversational agents lose their presence at home". *Implication:* a precedent for an onboarding and early-adoption framing, where decline in use is the finding, with N=8.
- **[Foundational] Cowan, B.R., Pantidi, N., Coyle, D., Morrissey, K., Clarke, P., Al-Shehri, S., Earley, D. & Bandeira, N. (2017). Both focus on infrequent or novice users' barriers and on how users recover from VUI obstacles (from my knowledge; metadata verified). *Implication:* related work for an onboarding framing. Light users' first drives are exactly this population.

**In-car speech and LLM agents (setting-specific)**

"An exploration of speech-based productivity support in the car." *CHI 2019*.** [doi:10.1145/3290605.3300494](https://doi.org/10.1145/3290605.3300494). ## 6. Common reviewer criticisms of small-n deployments and how authors pre-empt them

### Takeaway
The predictable criticisms are novelty effects, short duration, small or self-selected (WEIRD) samples, demand characteristics and researcher-built-system bias, researcher-as-participant, uneven engagement and attrition, lack of efficacy evidence, and, for LLM systems, inconsistent scepticism about the LLM itself. Each has a citable, field-accepted response. The usual pre-emption is to scope claims to formative understanding, report engagement and change logs transparently, separate early from later use, and cite methodological literature that reframes the issue.

### Cited Findings
- **Novelty effect.** **[Foundational] Koch, M., von Luck, K., Schwarzer, J. & Draheim, S. (2018). "The novelty effect in large display deployments – experiences and lessons-learned for evaluating prototypes." *ECSCW 2018*, EUSSET Reports.** [EUSSET DL](https://dl.eusset.eu/handle/20.500.12015/3115); [PDF](https://users.informatik.haw-hamburg.de/~ubicomp/arbeiten/papers/ecscw18.pdf).
  - Novelty is "predominantly present in two scenarios: (a) initially, after a system's deployment, and (b) when changes are made to the system's state".
  - It "took ten weeks to subside" in one project, but "one week… was sufficient for the usage patterns to stabilize" in another.
  - It cites Hazlewood et al.: "The four-week mark was [the timeframe] … for the initial novelty factor to wear off".
  - It links duration to whether the system is voluntary or integrated into operations.
  *Implication:* report usage by week and mark system-update dates. Present early versus later drives separately for the heavy user. Concede that light users' data are mostly novelty-phase, which suits an onboarding framing.
- **[Foundational] Hazlewood, W.R., Stolterman, E. & Connelly, K. (2011). "Issues in evaluating ambient displays in the wild: two case studies." *CHI 2011*.** [doi:10.1145/1978942.1979071](https://doi.org/10.1145/1978942.1979071). The source of the "four-week mark" heuristic quoted by Koch et al. *Implication:* about 4 weeks is a citable minimum for novelty to begin to fade, but only for people who used the system throughout.
- **Short duration.** Kjærup et al. 2021 and Kjeldskov & Skov 2014 (Section 1) argue that duration should match the "cycles inherent in the object and context". *Implication:* argue in terms of commute cycles: the heavy user had 36, the light users 1–9. Avoid the label "longitudinal".
- **Demand characteristics and researcher-built system bias.** **[Foundational] Dell, N., Vaidyanathan, V., Medhi, I., Cutrell, E. & Thies, W. (2012). "'Yours is better!': participant response bias in HCI." *CHI 2012*.** [doi:10.1145/2207676.2208589](https://doi.org/10.1145/2207676.2208589); [summary](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/?p=162695). Across 450 interviews, respondents were "about 2.5x more likely to prefer a technological artifact they believe to be developed by the interviewer". The figure rose to 5x with a foreign interviewer, and preference held even when the artifact was degraded. With Brown et al. 2011 (Section 2), this is the standard evidence. *Implication:* if the first author built Souffleur and also interviews, (i) say so, (ii) have someone else run some interviews or frame questions around specific incidents rather than evaluation, (iii) treat positive ratings sceptically, and (iv) foreground behavioural data (use, non-use, abandonment) over stated satisfaction.
- **Self-selection and WEIRD samples.** **[Foundational] Linxen, S., Sturm, C., Brühlmann, F., Cassau, V., Opwis, K. & Reinecke, K. (2021). "How WEIRD is CHI?" *CHI 2021*.** [doi:10.1145/3411764.3445488](https://doi.org/10.1145/3411764.3445488). "73% of CHI study findings are based on Western participant samples, representing less than 12% of the world's population", and samples skew educated and rich. *Implication:* report demographics, commute length and type, prior voice-AI use and recruitment channel. State the boundary of transferability: e.g., Danish or European car commuters who volunteered for a voice-AI study.
- **Researcher-as-participant.** **[Foundational] Neustaedter, C. & Sengers, P. (2012). "Autobiographical design in HCI research: designing and learning through use-it-yourself." *DIS 2012*.** [doi:10.1145/2317956.2318034](https://doi.org/10.1145/2317956.2318034). Defines autobiographical design as design research drawing on extensive, genuine use by those creating the system (from my knowledge; metadata verified). **[Current] Desjardins, A., Tomico, O., Lucero, A., Cecchinato, M.E. & Neustaedter, C. (2021). "Introduction to the special issue on first-person methods in HCI." *ACM TOCHI* 28(6).** [doi:10.1145/3492342](https://doi.org/10.1145/3492342). Distinguishes first-, second- and third-person methods and raises "boundaries, risk, and accountability" (abstract). *Implication:* exclude researcher, guest and test accounts from participant data. If the researcher's own extensive use informed design, report it separately and explicitly as first-person or autobiographical data, citing these papers, rather than mixing it in.
- **Uneven engagement and attrition.** Brown et al. 2011 (results depending "on a subset of trial participants") and Eysenbach 2005 (Section 2). *Implication:* publish the per-participant engagement table and theme incidence. Say plainly which claims rest on one participant.
- **"Where is the evidence it works?"** Klasnja et al. 2011 (Section 1); **[Foundational] Greenberg, S. & Buxton, B. (2008). "Usability evaluation considered harmful (some of the time)." *CHI 2008*.** [doi:10.1145/1357054.1357074](https://doi.org/10.1145/1357054.1357074). Argues that premature or inappropriate evaluation can stifle early-stage, novel ideas (from my knowledge; metadata verified). *Implication:* scope the claims to formative understanding and design knowledge. Make no efficacy claims about offloading or productivity.
- **Present-future gap.** **[Foundational] Salovaara, A., Oulasvirta, A. & Jacucci, G. (2017). "Evaluation of prototypes and the problem of possible futures." *CHI 2017*.** [doi:10.1145/3025453.3025658](https://doi.org/10.1145/3025453.3025658). "A prototype under study exists firmly in the present world… but its real context of use is a partially unknown future." "A carelessly designed evaluation may inadvertently evaluate the wrong futures, contexts, or user groups". *Implication:* state which future the study speaks to, for example everyday voice AI available in any car via phone, and which it does not, for example autonomous vehicles or always-on proactive agents. Justify why these participants are plausible early inhabitants of that future.
- **LLM-specific reviewer scepticism.** **[Current] Felix Navarro, K., Syriani, E. & Arawjo, I. (2026). "Reporting and reviewing LLM-integrated systems in HCI: challenges and considerations." *arXiv:2602.05128*.** [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.05128). Interviews with 18 authors of LLM-integrated system papers plus 6 experts find that "reviewers apply uniquely skeptical and inconsistent standards towards papers that report LLM-integrated systems". Authors respond by "add[ing] technical evaluations, justify[ing] usage, and de-emphasiz[ing] LLM presence". The paper finds prompt reporting "context-dependent". *Implication:* report model, version and prompting strategy at a level fit for the contribution. Justify why an LLM is needed. Keep the paper's centre of gravity on human experience, not model performance.
- **Safety while driving.** Martelaro et al. 2019 and Monk et al. 2026 (Section 5). *Implication:* acknowledge distraction risk, describe the safety design (hands-free, no screen use while moving) and the ethics approval, and cite on-road cognitive-load evidence. Do not make safety claims of your own.

### Inferences
- **Pre-emption checklist for the Limitations and Methods sections:**
  1. Scope claims as formative (Klasnja; Greenberg & Buxton).
  2. Plot weekly use and mark update dates to show novelty (Koch; Hazlewood).
  3. Disclose the author-built system and positionality, and prefer behavioural over evaluative evidence (Dell; Brown; CHI 2026 criteria).
  4. Report demographics and the transferability boundary (Linxen).
  5. Separate or exclude researcher and guest accounts (Neustaedter & Sengers; Desjardins et al.).
  6. Include a per-participant engagement and incidence matrix (Bowman; Brown).
  7. Justify the sample by information power and make no saturation claim (Malterud; Braun & Clarke).
  8. Avoid IRR, justified by McDonald et al. and Crabtree.
  9. Include a privacy-design rationale with an explicit statement of what cannot be known (LanguageLogger; Boeschoten; Mahmood 2026 as contrast).
  10. Report the LLM per Felix Navarro et al.
  11. Address driving safety (Martelaro; Monk).
- **The biggest single risk is overclaiming from the heavy user.** The safest strong framing combines two levels. First, a cross-participant onboarding and early-adoption account, which all participants contribute to. Second, an in-depth case of sustained use, from one participant, clearly labelled as such.

### Gaps
- I found no published analysis of actual IMWUT or CHI reviews that quantifies which criticisms small-n deployment papers most often receive. The list above is drawn from methods papers that name these threats, not from review corpora.
- No source gives a validated novelty-decay timeline for voice or conversational agents in particular. Cho et al. 2019 and Koch et al. 2018 suggest it varies from about one to ten or more weeks depending on context.
