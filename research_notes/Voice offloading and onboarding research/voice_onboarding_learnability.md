# Learning, onboarding, discoverability and adoption/abandonment of voice assistants and LLM voice agents

Scope note for the report writer: compiled 13 Oct 2026 for framing the Souffleur formative field study (voice AI thinking partner on car commutes; IMWUT/CHI-adjacent). Each entry gives authors, year, title, venue, link, peer-review status, a 1–2 sentence finding and a one-line *Implication* for Souffleur. Metadata (titles, authors, venues, years) for every DOI below was checked against Crossref and/or Semantic Scholar on 13 Oct 2026. Findings come from verified abstracts unless an entry says otherwise. Where a detail comes only from a search-engine summary or from recall of the full text, the entry says so. "[PR]" means peer-reviewed and "[Preprint]" means arXiv only, venue not confirmed. "[Grey]" means practitioner or non-archival.

## Q1. Foundational findings on voice assistant use and expectations (discoverability, mental models, abandonment)

### Takeaway
The pre-LLM literature agrees on one story. People's expectations of voice assistants are badly out of step with what the systems can do (a "gulf"). Users find capabilities by guessing and trial-and-error rather than through any discovery mechanism, explore little after the first weeks, and settle on a few simple commands. Many people limit their use or abandon the assistant, mainly because they "cannot find valuable uses". The literature mostly treats discoverability as a property of the interface. It rarely studies it as a learning process that unfolds over weeks in situ.

### Cited Findings

**Expectation gap and mental models**
- **Luger, E. & Sellen, A. (2016). "Like Having a Really Bad PA": The Gulf between User Expectation and Experience of Conversational Agents. CHI '16. [PR, foundational]** Interviews with 14 conversational-agent users (Siri, Google Now, Cortana). The authors found "user expectations dramatically out of step with the operation of the systems, particularly in terms of known machine intelligence, system capability and goals", and framed this with Norman's gulfs of execution and evaluation. Two further details come from recall of the full text and should be checked before quoting: users explored capabilities through playful or low-stakes use, and they fell back on a narrow set of simple tasks. *Implication:* this gives the canonical framing for Souffleur's "what can it do / is it working" problems as gulfs of execution and evaluation in an eyes-free setting. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/2858036.2858288); [MSR page](https://microsoft.com/en-us/research/?p=285179)
- **Cowan, B.R., Pantidi, N., Coyle, D., Morrissey, K., Clarke, P., Al-Shehri, S., Earley, D., Bandeira, N. (2017). "What can I help you with?": Infrequent Users' Experiences of Intelligent Personal Assistants. MobileHCI '17. [PR, foundational]** A qualitative study of people who tried intelligent personal assistants but chose not to use them regularly. Infrequent users were frustrated by the limits of hands-free interaction. Social embarrassment and comparisons with human assistants (the humanness of the assistant "juxtaposing their limitations") weighed heavily, as did privacy, data-permanency and transparency concerns. The authors call for "designing for interruptability" and for rethinking the human metaphor. *Implication:* most Souffleur participants made 1–9 drives, so they are "infrequent users" in Cowan's sense, and this paper names the reasons light users give that are not about learnability. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/3098279.3098539)
- **Clark, L., Pantidi, N., Cooney, O., Doyle, P., Garaialde, D., Edwards, J., Spillane, B., et al. (2019). What Makes a Good Conversation? Challenges in Designing Truly Conversational Agents. CHI '19. [PR]** Metadata verified; the finding is from recall. Participants framed conversational agents as transactional tools rather than social partners. *Implication:* the "thinking partner" framing raises expectations, which Khadpe et al. (below) suggest can lower adoption. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/3290605.3300705)
- **Doyle, P.R., Clark, L., Cowan, B.R. (2021). What Do We See in Them? Identifying Dimensions of Partner Models for Speech Interfaces Using a Psycholexical Approach. CHI '21. [PR]** A "partner model" is the user's perception of a speech system's competence and communicative ability. The paper identifies three dimensions: (1) competence and dependability, (2) human-likeness, and (3) cognitive flexibility. It stresses that these perceptions are dynamic. *Implication:* partner models offer a ready-made construct for "what the user believes the agent can hold or do", and that belief is exactly what onboarding has to shape. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/3411764.3445206)
- **Khadpe, P., Krishna, R., Fei-Fei, L., Hancock, J., Bernstein, M. (2020). Conceptual Metaphors Impact Perceptions of Human-AI Collaboration. CSCW (PACM HCI). [PR]** The study used a Wizard-of-Oz agent whose behaviour stayed identical while its metaphor changed (N=260). Metaphors signalling low competence led to better evaluations than high-competence ones, and intention to adopt fell as projected competence rose. *Implication:* how Souffleur is introduced (e.g., as a "thinking partner" versus a "notetaker") is itself an onboarding manipulation that could be studied. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/3415234)
- **Desai, S. & Twidale, M. (2023). Metaphors in Voice User Interfaces: A Slippery Fish. ACM TOCHI. [PR]** Metaphors for voice interfaces held by designers and users are "contextually fluid" and change with the mode of conversation. Some metaphors are unhelpful. *Implication:* expect participants' metaphors for Souffleur (dictaphone? passenger? assistant?) to shift over time and between modes such as monologue and dialogue. Those shifts can be coded. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/3609326)

**Discoverability and learnability of voice interfaces**
- **Myers, C., Furqan, A., Nebolsky, J., Caro, K., Zhu, J. (2018). Patterns for How Users Overcome Obstacles in Voice User Interfaces. CHI '18. [PR, foundational]** The details here come from the Drexel repository summary, not the full text. Twelve users worked with a voice-interface calendar (DiscoverCal) over three sessions. The authors found four obstacle types: *Unfamiliar Intent* (the system cannot parse the utterance, or the user attempts an unsupported intent), *NLP Error*, *Failed Feedback* and *System Error*. NLP errors were the most frequent, but the other obstacles were more likely to frustrate or confuse. Users preferred *guessing* over visual aids or recall. *Implication:* the obstacle taxonomy maps directly onto Souffleur data. The "stop recording" request is an Unfamiliar Intent, and "can't tell if it's working" is Failed Feedback. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/3173574.3173580); [Drexel record](https://researchdiscovery.drexel.edu/esploro/outputs/conferenceProceeding/Patterns-for-How-Users-Overcome-Obstacles/991019168451704721)
- **Myers, C., Furqan, A., Zhu, J. (2019). The Impact of User Characteristics and Preferences on Performance with an Unfamiliar Voice User Interface. CHI '19. [PR]** n=50 on DiscoverCal. Programming experience had little effect on performance, but *assimilation bias* (carrying over expectations from other voice interfaces) did. Technically confident users relied on trial-and-error, and users who wanted more guidance showed "cautious" performance patterns. *Implication:* prior use of Siri, Alexa or ChatGPT voice will shape what Souffleur participants try, so screening for prior voice and LLM use is worthwhile. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/3290605.3300277)
- **Furqan, A., Myers, C., Zhu, J. (2017). Learnability through Adaptive Discovery Tools in Voice User Interfaces. CHI EA '17. [PR, extended abstract]** Introduces DiscoverCal, a voice interface with an *adaptive* discovery tool. It adapts to contextual relevance and user performance "to ensure that learnability … extends beyond initial use"; this detail comes from the Drexel thesis abstract. *Implication:* this is an early precedent for adapting capability hints over time, the same idea behind proposing macros when repetition is detected. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/3027063.3053166); [thesis DOI](https://doi.org/10.17918/etd-7358)
- **Myers, C. (2019). Adaptive Suggestions to Increase Learnability for Voice User Interfaces. IUI '19 Companion (doctoral consortium). [PR, short]** Proposes adapting a voice interface's *spoken* feedback to suggest commands when users hit errors, tailored to detected proficiency and predicted goal. *Implication:* this is the closest prior proposal to voice-only, in-dialogue teaching triggered by errors (an error-driven "teachable moment"). — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/3308557.3308727)
- **Kirschthaler, P., Porcheron, M., Fischer, J.E. (2020). What Can I Say? Effects of Discoverability in VUIs on Task Performance and User Experience. CUI '20. [PR]** Wizard-of-Oz food ordering compared automatic informational prompts, help on request ("What can I say?") and a baseline with neither. Both strategies beat the baseline on task performance and usability, with no significant difference between them. Most participants said they would prefer on-request help for frequent use. The authors highlight the need to distinguish initial use from longer-term use. *Implication:* this is direct evidence that discovery strategy should change as use goes on (proactive early, on-demand later). It is a lab study and never tested longitudinally, which is a gap Souffleur can address. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/3405755.3406119)
- **Corbett, E. & Weber, A. (2016). What Can I Say? Addressing User Experience Challenges of a Mobile Voice User Interface for Accessibility. MobileHCI '16. [PR]** Only the metadata is verified. The title and venue confirm it addresses the "what can I say" discoverability challenge in a mobile accessibility voice interface; the detailed findings were not fetched. *Implication:* cite it alongside Kirschthaler for the "what can I say" problem. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/2935334.2935386)
- **Porcheron, M., Fischer, J.E., Reeves, S., Sharples, S. (2018). Voice Interfaces in Everyday Life. CHI '18. [PR, foundational]** Conversation analysis of month-long Amazon Echo deployments. Voice-interface use is "finely coordinated with the sequential organisation of talk" and threaded into other ongoing activities. The paper raises implications for the *accountability* of voice interaction and for request and response design, and questions the notion of "conversational" interfaces. *Implication:* "is it still working?" during silences is an accountability problem. A system that cannot display its state cannot be held accountable inside the flow of talk, and in a car that flow runs alongside driving. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/3173574.3174214)
- **Beneteau, E., Richards, O.K., Zhang, M., Kientz, J.A., Yip, J., Hiniker, A. (2019). Communication Breakdowns Between Families and Alexa. CHI '19. [PR]** A four-week Echo Dot deployment with 10 families yielded 59 breakdowns. Repair relied on "discourse scaffolding" and speech modifications, and Alexa's responses shaped which repair strategies families used. *Implication:* system responses teach users how to repair, so every Souffleur response, including silence, is also an onboarding message. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/3290605.3300473)

**Long-term usage logs and abandonment**
- **Bentley, F., Luvogt, C., Silverman, M., Wirasinghe, R., White, B., Lottridge, D. (2018). Understanding the Long-Term Use of Smart Speaker Assistants. IMWUT 2(3). [PR, foundational]** Logs of 65,499 interactions from 88 Google Home households over an average of 110 days. Commands in some domains *grew longer over time* as people tried new phrasings, "yet exploration of new topics was low". Four distinct user groups emerged from time-of-day and category patterns. *Implication:* this is the IMWUT precedent for log-based analysis of use over time. Low exploration of new capabilities is the expected baseline Souffleur should test against, and Souffleur's spread of 1 to 36 drives fits a "user group" analysis. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/3264901)
- **Sciuto, A., Saini, A., Forlizzi, J., Hong, J.I. (2018). "Hey Alexa, What's Up?": Mixed-Methods Studies of In-Home Conversational Agent Usage. DIS '18. [PR]** Combined history logs from 75 Alexa users (more than 278,000 commands) with 7 in-home interviews, covering behaviour while acclimating to Alexa, device placement and daily use patterns. *Implication:* this is a methodological precedent for pairing logs with interviews to study how people acclimate. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/3196709.3196772)
- **Ammari, T., Kaye, J., Tsai, J.Y., Bentley, F. (2019). Music, Search, and IoT: How People (Really) Use Voice Assistants. ACM TOCHI 26(3). [PR]** Nineteen interviews plus logs from 82 Alexa devices (193,665 commands) and 88 Google Home devices (65,499 commands). Use concentrated on music, search and IoT control. *Implication:* use of commercial voice assistants converges on a few simple domains. An open-ended "thinking partner" has no such default use and so needs onboarding more. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/3311956)
- **Cho, M., Lee, S., Lee, K.-P. (2019). Once a Kind Friend is Now a Thing: Understanding How Conversational Agents at Home are Forgotten. DIS '19. [PR]** A 12-week study of eight first-time Echo households using diaries, surveys and interviews. It traces "how conversational agents lose their presence at home" and shows that the voice interface, physical form and installation shape expectations. *Implication:* this paper frames decline as fading *presence*. Souffleur has no physical presence at all (it only exists when a drive starts), so starting a drive is the only reminder that it exists. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/3322276.3322332)
- **Trajkova, M. & Martin-Hammond, A. (2020). "Alexa is a Toy": Exploring Older Adults' Reasons for Using, Limiting, and Abandoning Echo. CHI '20. [PR, foundational]** Eight focus groups with 38 older adults, 36 of whom had owned an Echo for at least a year. "Over time, most participants became non-users". The reasons were "difficulty finding valuable uses, beliefs associated with ability and IVA use, or challenges with use in shared spaces." *Implication:* "Difficulty finding valuable uses" sits halfway between not learning the system and not needing it. That makes it the key construct for separating the two in Souffleur. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/3313831.3376760)
- **Pradhan, A., Lazar, A., Findlater, L. (2020). Use of Intelligent Voice Assistants by Older Adults with Low Technology Use. ACM TOCHI 27(4). [PR]** A three-week Echo Dot deployment with seven older adults. Some use dropped over the three weeks (e.g., music), while information seeking stayed steady. Memory features such as timers and reminders were initially seen as useful, but actual use was "unexpectedly low due to reliability concerns." *Implication:* perceived reliability, not discoverability, can suppress use of exactly the "offloading" features. That points to a third non-use category beyond "didn't learn" and "didn't need": "didn't trust". — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/3373759)
- **Kim, S. & Choudhury, A. (2021). Exploring Older Adults' Perception and Use of Smart Speaker-Based Voice Assistants: A Longitudinal Study. Computers in Human Behavior 124. [PR]** The details here come from a search summary, not the full text. Twelve older adults used Google Home for 16 weeks. Attitudes shifted from enjoying the simplicity and convenience of voice to later concerns with avoiding errors and building meaningful companionship. *Implication:* learning goals change over weeks, from "how to talk to it" toward "what it is for me". — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2021.106914); [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0747563221002375)
- **Upadhyay, P., Heung, S., Azenkot, S., Brewer, R.N. (2023). Studying Exploration & Long-Term Use of Voice Assistants by Older Adults. CHI '23. [PR]** Ten older adults in long-term care who had used Alexa for at least a year. Participants "learned to use Alexa through a training program that encouraged exploration", and they sustained use for routines, mood, cognitive stimulation and socialisation. The paper proposes strategies to promote voice-assistant learning and adoption. Note that the authors are Upadhyay et al.; some search tools misattribute the paper. *Implication:* this is rare evidence that *structured, exploration-oriented onboarding* is associated with sustained use. It is retrospective and in a care setting. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/3544548.3580925)

### Inferences
- The classic findings map closely onto Souffleur's early data. The false starts (20 drives of ~18 s) are an *initial learnability* failure in Grossman's sense (see Q5). "Can't tell whether it's working" is Myers' *Failed Feedback* and Porcheron's accountability problem. Asking to stop recording by voice is an *Unfamiliar Intent*. The long tail of 1–9 drives matches Cowan's infrequent users and Trajkova's "difficulty finding valuable uses".
- Pre-LLM voice assistants failed largely because their *command space* was narrow and invisible. An LLM agent turns this around: the input space is almost unconstrained, but the *action/capability space* (what it can actually do: stop recording, build a macro, keep state) is still narrow and invisible. The discoverability problem moves from "what phrasing works" to "what actions exist and what it can hold". That shift is the conceptual bridge to a 2026 contribution.

### Gaps
- None of the foundational studies examines discoverability *in a car* or *during a primary task over weeks*. All are home, phone or lab studies.
- I could not access the full texts of Luger & Sellen, Myers et al. 2018 or Corbett & Weber. Details beyond the abstracts and repository summaries should be checked against the PDFs before quoting.

## Q2. Recent work (2023–2026) on learning LLM-based assistants: gulf of envisioning, capability discovery, mental models, expectations of LLM voice agents, tutorials vs learning by doing, feedforward

### Takeaway
The LLM literature has renamed the old gulf. The "gulf of envisioning" (Subramonyam et al., CHI 2024) and the "metacognitive demands" of generative AI (Tankelevitch et al., CHI 2024) both say users do not know what to ask, how to ask it, or what to expect. Empirically, users skip tutorials and learn by doing, and early trajectories lock in. Log data from 2026 shows LLM users explore *less* than the open input space would suggest. Users also over-estimate LLM accuracy. Work on *LLM voice* assistants specifically is thin. It consists mostly of short lab and home studies, often with older adults, and I found no peer-reviewed study of how people learn ChatGPT Voice, Gemini Live or Alexa+ in the wild.

### Cited Findings

**Theory: envisioning, metacognition, prompting**
- **Subramonyam, H., Pea, R., Pondoc, C., Agrawala, M., Seifert, C. (2024). Bridging the Gulf of Envisioning: Cognitive Challenges in Prompt Based Interactions with LLMs. CHI '24. [PR]** Extends Norman's gulfs with a gulf of *envisioning* made of three misalignments in what users don't know: (1) what the task should be, (2) how to instruct the LLM and (3) what to expect of the output. *Implication:* this is the primary theoretical hook. Souffleur adds a fourth misalignment for agents that act, "what the agent can *do* and *hold*", and makes the gulf harder to bridge because there is no screen. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/3613904.3642754); [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.14459)
- **Tankelevitch, L., Kewenig, V., Simkute, A., Scott, A.E., Sarkar, A., Sellen, A., Rintel, S. (2024). The Metacognitive Demands and Opportunities of Generative AI. CHI '24. [PR]** Generative AI imposes high demands on users' metacognitive monitoring and control (prompting, evaluating, relying, fitting it into workflows). The authors propose metacognitive support strategies built into systems. *Implication:* this links onboarding to the offloading literature. Learning a thinking partner is largely learning to monitor what to delegate and when to check. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/3613904.3642902)
- **Zamfirescu-Pereira, J.D., Wong, R.Y., Hartmann, B., Yang, Q. (2023). Why Johnny Can't Prompt: How Non-AI Experts Try (and Fail) to Design LLM Prompts. CHI '23. [PR]** Non-experts explored prompts "opportunistically, not systematically". Expectations carried over from instructing humans, and a tendency to overgeneralise from single examples, were barriers. *Implication:* Souffleur users will likely overgeneralise from a single drive (e.g., one failed macro offer leads them to decide "it can't do that"). — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/3544548.3581388)
- **Simkute, A., Tankelevitch, L., Kewenig, V., Scott, A.E., Sellen, A., Rintel, S. (2024/25). Ironies of Generative AI: Understanding and Mitigating Productivity Loss in Human-AI Interaction. International Journal of Human–Computer Interaction. [PR]** Applies the "ironies of automation" to generative AI. Users shift from producing to evaluating, workflows get restructured unhelpfully, interruptions increase, and easy tasks get easier while hard ones get harder. *Implication:* proactive offers and macro suggestions can be *interruptions*, and this paper is the human-factors grounding for that risk while driving. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/10447318.2024.2405782)
- **Mahdavi Goloujeh, A., Sullivan, A., Magerko, B. (2024). Is It AI or Is It Me? Understanding Users' Prompt Journey with Text-to-Image Generative AI Tools. CHI '24. [PR]** Metadata verified; the finding is inferred from the title and not fetched. The paper traces how users attribute failures to themselves versus the AI over a "prompt journey". *Implication:* the same attribution question applies when Souffleur users judge whether a failure was theirs or the system's. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/3613904.3642861)

**Learning by doing vs tutorials; early trajectories**
- **Gautam, K., Bhatia, P., Chilana, P.K. (2026). "I Just Need GPT to Refine My Prompts": Rethinking Onboarding and Help-Seeking with Generative 3D Modelling Tools. CHI '26. [PR]** An observational study with 26 participants (14 casual users, 12 professionals). Participants "skipped tutorials and manuals, relying on trial and error". "The prompt box became the entry point for learning, collapsing onboarding into immediate action". Casual users turned to *other* LLMs for help ("recursive AI-for-AI support"). *Implication:* this is the most recent peer-reviewed onboarding study for generative tools, and it is screen-based. Souffleur's equivalent of the prompt box is the open microphone: onboarding collapses into the first drive. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/3772318.3791124)
- **Zhu, S., Rzeszotarski, J.M., Mimno, D. (2026). Priming, Path-dependence, and Plasticity: Understanding the Molding of User-LLM Interaction and Its Implications from (Many) Chat Logs in the Wild. arXiv:2605.05767. [Preprint]** An analysis of 140K sessions from 7,955 users over time. "Interaction patterns form and stabilize rapidly through individual early trajectories". Longitudinal outcomes, including *retention*, "are strongly correlated with early exploration". The authors describe an "agency paradox": despite unconstrained input, users explore less. *Implication:* this is strong motivation that the first 1–3 drives are where onboarding matters. It also supports analysing Souffleur's early-drive exploration as a predictor of later use, a hypothesis to probe with N≈6. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.05767)
- **Liu, F. (2024). New Users Need Support with Generative-AI Tools. Nielsen Norman Group. [Grey]** A qualitative usability study with six participants on three Chinese chatbots. New users asked bots *about their own capabilities* ("Can you…" prompts), which NN/g says it also saw in US ChatGPT studies. Long up-front tutorials overwhelmed users. Recommendations: contextual help tied to actions, general rather than niche examples, and "ensure bots understand their own capabilities and assist proactively". *Implication:* users naturally try to onboard *conversationally*. A voice agent that cannot answer "what can you do?" accurately loses the main channel users reach for. — [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/new-AI-users-onboarding/)
- **Tie, J., Yao, B., Li, T., Fang, H., Ahmed, S.I., Wang, D. (2024). "Should I Give Up Now?" Investigating LLM Pitfalls in Software Engineering. arXiv:2411.09916. [Preprint]** 17 of 26 participants abandoned ChatGPT within a task. Unhelpful responses raised the likelihood of abandonment roughly elevenfold. *Implication:* abandonment *within a session* follows an effort–benefit calculation, so early failed drives are likely the strongest predictor of dropout. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.09916)

**Mental models and calibration of LLM capability**
- **Steyvers, M., Tejeda, H., Kumar, A., Belem, C., Karny, S., Hu, X., Mayer, L., Smyth, P. (2025). What Large Language Models Know and What People Think They Know. Nature Machine Intelligence. [PR]** Users over-estimate LLM accuracy when given default explanations (the "calibration gap"), and longer explanations raise confidence without raising accuracy. Explanations that reflect the model's internal confidence narrow the gap. *Implication:* users' partner models of what Souffleur "holds" will probably be over-confident, which supports onboarding framed as *calibration*. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1038/s42256-024-00976-7); [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.13835)
- **Gero, K.I., Ashktorab, Z., Dugan, C., Pan, Q., Johnson, J., Geyer, W., Ruiz, M., et al. (2020). Mental Models of AI Agents in a Cooperative Game Setting. CHI '20. [PR, pre-LLM]** People who won more had better estimates of the AI's abilities. Understanding the technology was not enough on its own; people also had to analyse the agent's behaviour. *Implication:* capability mental models come from observing behaviour, which argues for designing Souffleur to *show its work* (see Q3). — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/3313831.3376316)
- **Doyle, P.R., Gessinger, I., Edwards, J., Clark, L., Dumbleton, O., Garaialde, D., Rough, D., et al. (2025). The Partner Modelling Questionnaire. ACM TOCHI. [PR]** See Q5. It operationalises partner models of speech agents with test–retest reliability over 4 and 12 weeks, which makes it directly usable for tracking how mental models change longitudinally. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/3729170)

**LLM voice assistants specifically**
- **Mahmood, A., Wang, J., Yao, B., Wang, D., Huang, C.-M. (2025). User Interaction Patterns and Breakdowns in Conversing with LLM-Powered Voice Assistants. International Journal of Human-Computer Studies. [PR]** (arXiv 2023.) N=20 used a ChatGPT-powered voice assistant (an Alexa skill) for three scenarios: medical self-diagnosis, creative planning and discussion. The LLM elicited richer interaction patterns, and "LLMs absorb the majority of VA intent recognition failures". *Implication:* LLMs hide NLU failures, which can make *capability* failures harder to see. The agent answers fluently even when it cannot act (e.g., "stop recording"). — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijhcs.2024.103406)
- **Mahmood, A., Wang, J., Huang, C.-M. (2026). Situated Understanding of Errors in Older Adults' Interactions with Voice Assistants: A Month-Long, In-Home Study. ACM Transactions on Accessible Computing. [PR]** (arXiv 2024; Crossref gives the issue year as 2026.) Fifteen homes had smart speakers with custom audio recorders, and a ChatGPT-powered voice assistant was deployed midway through. LLMs helped prevent and manage errors, but "interaction-level challenges still remain". *Implication:* this is the closest methodological precedent: a month-long in-situ audio capture of an LLM voice agent, analysed for breakdowns. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/3796236); [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.02421)
- **Mahmood, A., Cao, S., Stiber, M., Antony, V.N., Huang, C.-M. (2025). Voice Assistants for Health Self-Management: Designing for and with Older Adults. CHI '25. [PR]** A GPT-4 Alexa prototype. A search summary reports a limitation the authors point out: commercial voice assistants treat each input as standalone and do not remember or learn routines, which undermines recurring tasks. *Implication:* Souffleur's macros and persistent board address exactly this gap, but users' *prior* models of voice assistants as stateless will need to be overwritten (assimilation bias; Myers 2019). — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/3706598.3713839)
- **Fang, C.M., Liu, A.R., Danry, V., Lee, E., Chan, S.W.T., Pataranutaporn, P., et al. (2025). How AI and Human Behaviors Shape Psychosocial Effects of Extended Chatbot Use: A Longitudinal Randomized Controlled Study. arXiv:2503.17473. [Preprint]** A four-week RCT (n=981, more than 300k messages) comparing text, neutral voice and engaging voice. Condition had no significant psychosocial effect, while *voluntary heavier use* correlated with worse outcomes. *Implication:* it is a rare multi-week study of voice versus text LLM use, and it warns that "more use" is not automatically the success metric. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.17473)
- **Industry agents (ChatGPT Advanced Voice, Gemini Live, Alexa+).** Searches turned up only press coverage and one accessibility study of ChatGPT live *video* for blind and low-vision users (arXiv:2508.03651; not reviewed in depth). I found no peer-reviewed study of how ordinary users learn or calibrate these products. — [arXiv 2508.03651](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.03651)

**Feedforward**
- **Vermeulen, J., Luyten, K., van den Hoven, E., Coninx, K. (2013). Crossing the Bridge over Norman's Gulf of Execution: Revealing Feedforward's True Identity. CHI '13. [PR, foundational]** The metadata is verified; the summary is from recall. Feedforward tells users what the *result* of an action will be before they act, as distinct from affordances and feedback. *Implication:* this supplies the vocabulary for spoken previews such as "If you say 'save that', I'll add it to your board". — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/2470654.2466255)
- **Min, B. & Xia, H. (2025). Feedforward in Generative AI: Opportunities for a Design Space. arXiv:2502.14229. [Preprint]** Users "struggle to anticipate what AI will generate". The authors propose feedforward designs for conversational UIs, editors, malleable interfaces and *automation agents*. *Implication:* feedforward for automation agents is the closest analogue to previewing a macro. A spoken, screen-free feedforward design remains unexplored. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.14229)

### Inferences
- Taken together (Gautam 2026; Zhu 2026; NN/g 2024), the 2024–26 evidence says onboarding to generative AI *is* the first few sessions, whatever tutorials exist. For Souffleur this means the first drives should be analysed as the onboarding, and the 18-second false starts are failed onboarding events rather than noise.
- LLMs widen the gulf of evaluation for *agentic* capabilities. Fluent answers hide whether the agent did anything, as Mahmood et al. 2025 found when LLMs absorbed intent failures. Combined with the calibration gap (Steyvers 2025), this predicts over-trust in what Souffleur "holds", not only under-use.

### Gaps
- I found no peer-reviewed in-the-wild study of how users learn ChatGPT Voice, Gemini Live or Alexa+. That evidence is limited to press and grey literature.
- I found no paper that extends the gulf of envisioning to *voice-only* or *eyes-busy* settings.
- I did not find a peer-reviewed CHI 2024–25 study of "mental models of LLM agents" for general users beyond those listed. A targeted ACM DL search may surface more.

## Q3. Conversational / in-dialogue onboarding, progressive disclosure, proactive capability suggestions, "teachable moments"; learning while doing another task (driving, cooking)

### Takeaway
Agents that teach their own capabilities have precedents. These include chatbot feature-conveying strategies, error-triggered spoken suggestions, the "What can I say?" strategy, software command recommenders and skill recommendation, and LLM-generated proactive question suggestions. However, they have been studied in the lab, in text, or on screens. In-car voice work since 2024 is almost entirely about the *timing and acceptability* of proactive suggestions, not about using proactivity to teach capabilities. Learning while doing another task is hard: in AAA work, practice over a week did not remove the cognitive cost of in-vehicle voice interaction. "Teachable moment" as a named construct in voice user interfaces yielded nothing.

### Inferences
- Combining Kirschthaler (proactive early, on-demand later), Du et al. (strong inference plus confirmation), White (offer "when needed, not obtrusive"), Hamid et al. (defer when the context is busy) and Strayer (practice does not erase in-car cost) gives a coherent design hypothesis. Capability teaching for Souffleur could be split into three channels. During the drive, the agent would give brief confirm-style offers only. At natural pauses, it would answer "what can you do?" on request. After the drive, the board would carry the fuller "here is what I did / what I could do" review. This is testable and has not yet been tested.
- Macro proposals have two functions: they offer automation, and they *reveal a capability* (discoverability). This double role is under-theorised. The command-recommender literature (CommunityCommands, White) treats recommendations as discovery but does not consider voice or eyes-busy settings.

### Gaps
- I found no study that explicitly uses "teachable moments" as a construct for voice interfaces. Error-triggered suggestion (Myers 2019) is the nearest.
- I found no empirical study of *progressive disclosure* of an LLM voice agent's capabilities over weeks.
- I found no study of an agent proposing reusable macros by voice and of whether users adopt or understand them.
- The in-car proactivity work is mostly simulator, VR or online-survey based. On-road, multi-week deployments of LLM in-car agents appear absent; I found none.

## Q4. Longitudinal deployments: novelty effects, habit formation, sustained use; telling "didn't learn it" from "didn't need it"

### Takeaway
Weeks-long deployments consistently show early decline (novelty), low exploration of new capabilities, and a subset of users who settle into stable routines. The reasons for abandonment are mixed. They include not finding valuable uses, reliability and trust concerns, social context, and effort–benefit imbalance. The non-use literature provides vocabulary for distinguishing kinds of non-use, but I found no validated method for separating "didn't learn" from "didn't need" in voice-agent studies. Doing so would require triangulating logs (attempted versus never-attempted capabilities), probes and interviews.

### Cited Findings
- **Novelty decline**
  - Pradhan et al. (2020): some usage dropped over three weeks while information seeking persisted — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/3373759).
  - Cho et al. (2019): agents "lose their presence" over 12 weeks — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/3322276.3322332).
  - Kim & Choudhury (2021): attitudes shifted over 16 weeks — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2021.106914).
  - Bentley et al. (2018): low topic exploration across about 110 days — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/3264901).
- **Early trajectories predict retention (LLMs).** Zhu, Rzeszotarski & Mimno (2026, preprint) found that retention correlates with early exploration and that patterns stabilise fast — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.05767).
- **Sustained use is possible with exploration-oriented training.** Upadhyay et al. (2023) — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/3544548.3580925).
- **LLM voice in multi-week field studies**
  - Mahmood et al. (2026), month-long in-home study — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/3796236).
  - **Mahmood, A., Kim, B., Zhao, H., Atwood, M.E., Buenaver, L.F., Smith, M.T., Huang, C.-M. (2026). Better Adherence, Richer Context: A Field Evaluation of LLM-Powered Conversational Voice Diaries for Sleep. arXiv:2606.18596. [Preprint]** A four-week between-subjects study (N=30) comparing a proactive LLM smart-speaker diary with a text diary. The voice diary had *higher adherence* and richer context, and was "easier to integrate into daily routines" despite longer perceived completion times. Voice intake produced lower completeness for structured fields. *Implication:* proactive voice prompts can sustain use over four weeks. The richness-versus-structure trade-off mirrors the step of organising talk into a board. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.18596)
  - Fang et al. (2025, preprint), four-week RCT including voice modes — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.17473).
- **Proactive agent over 14 days.** Users negotiated, built shared mental models, or resisted and disengaged (Abbas et al. 2025, preprint) — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.24073).
- **Abandonment reasons**
  - Trajkova & Martin-Hammond (2020): difficulty finding valuable uses, beliefs about ability, shared spaces — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/3313831.3376760).
  - Cowan et al. (2017): embarrassment, privacy, the human metaphor — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/3098279.3098539).
  - Tie et al. (2024, preprint): abandonment driven by effort and benefit — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.09916).
- **Vocabulary for non-use**
  - **Satchell, C. & Dourish, P. (2009). Beyond the User: Use and Non-Use in HCI. OzCHI '09. [PR, foundational]** The metadata is verified; the summary is from recall. Non-use takes multiple forms (lagging adoption, active resistance, disenchantment, disenfranchisement, displacement, disinterest) and should be studied in its own right. *Implication:* this is a typology for coding Souffleur's light users. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/1738826.1738829)
  - **Baumer, E.P.S., Adams, P., Khovanskaya, V.D., Liao, T.C., Smith, M.E., Schwanda Sosik, V., Williams, K. (2013). Limiting, Leaving, and (Re)lapsing: An Exploration of Facebook Non-Use Practices. CHI '13. [PR, foundational]** The metadata is verified; the summary is from recall. Non-use is not binary. It includes limiting, leaving and relapsing. *Implication:* Souffleur participants may be "limiting" (using it on some commutes only) rather than abandoning it. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/2470654.2466446)
- **Habit and continuance theory**
  - **Limayem, M., Hirt, S.G., Cheung, C.M.K. (2007). How Habit Limits the Predictive Power of Intention: The Case of Information Systems Continuance. MIS Quarterly 31(4). [PR, foundational]** — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.2307/25148817)
  - **Bhattacherjee, A. (2001). Understanding Information Systems Continuance: An Expectation-Confirmation Model. MIS Quarterly 25(3). [PR, foundational]** The summary is from recall. Continuance depends on whether initial expectations are confirmed and on perceived usefulness. *Implication:* this ties onboarding (setting expectations) directly to continued use (Kocielnik et al. 2019 show experimentally that setting expectations raises acceptance; see Q6). — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.2307/3250921)
- **Commuting gives a stable cue that could support habit formation**, but no voice-assistant study I found tests commute-anchored habit formation. The habit measures are in Q5.

### Inferences
- **Proposed operationalisation for separating "didn't learn" from "didn't need" (inference, not from a source).** The proposal triangulates across four signals. (a) *Attempt logs*: did the participant ever attempt a capability, even unsupported ones like "stop recording"? Attempts with failure suggest a learning or capability gap, while no attempts plus a low stated need suggest no need. (b) *Awareness probes*: at a mid-study check-in, ask "what can Souffleur do?" (recall) and then show a list (recognition). If participants are unaware of capabilities they say they would value, that is a learnability failure. (c) *Need inventory*: a short diary of moments on the commute when the participant wished they had captured or offloaded something. (d) *Non-use coding*: code light users with the Satchell & Dourish and Baumer categories, plus Pradhan's "didn't trust".
- Souffleur's distribution (one person with 36 drives, most with 1–9, one with 20 false starts) suggests at least three trajectories: adopter, limiter or leaver, and person who struggled to onboard. Bentley et al. found distinct user groups in voice-assistant logs, which gives precedent for describing such groups even at small N.

### Gaps
- I found no validated instrument for "didn't learn versus didn't need" in voice or AI studies.
- I found no longitudinal study of *in-car* LLM voice agents over weeks.
- The novelty-effect literature for AI tools is mostly informal. I did not locate a specific methodological paper on correcting for novelty in deployments of AI voice agents. The UIST 2025 workshop on longitudinal AI studies (Long et al., arXiv:2508.10252) states that one-time evaluations are insufficient because "user interactions with AI evolve over time through learning, adaptation, and repurposing" — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.10252).

## Q5. Measures and instruments for learnability, onboarding and adoption

### Takeaway
There is a usable toolkit, but almost none of it was built for eyes-free LLM agents. The pieces are a learnability taxonomy (Grossman et al., 2009: initial versus extended learnability), standard usability scales (SUS with its learnability items, UMUX-LITE), a speech-specific scale with a "habitability" factor (SASSI), a 2025 validated scale of speech-agent partner models with 4- and 12-week test–retest reliability (PMQ), and habit and continuance measures (SRBAI/SRHI, UTAUT2, ECM). Log-based indicators such as command growth, exploration breadth and obstacle coding complement them.

### Cited Findings
- **Grossman, T., Fitzmaurice, G., Attar, R. (2009). A Survey of Software Learnability: Metrics, Methodologies and Guidelines. CHI '09. [PR, foundational]** The summary is from recall. The paper reviews learnability definitions and distinguishes *initial learnability* (first-use performance) from *extended learnability* (change in performance over time). It proposes a taxonomy of learnability metrics and a "question-suggestion" protocol. *Implication:* the core framing for Souffleur. False starts are failures of initial learnability, and growth in capability use over weeks measures extended learnability. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/1518701.1518803)
- **Lewis, J.R. & Sauro, J. (2009). The Factor Structure of the System Usability Scale. HCII 2009 (LNCS 5619). [PR]** The summary is from recall. It identifies a two-item "Learnable" subscale in the SUS (items 4 and 10). Caveat: later work by the same authors questioned whether this factor structure is stable, so treat it as indicative. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-642-02806-9_12)
- **Lewis, J.R., Utesch, B.S., Maher, D.E. (2013). UMUX-LITE: When There's No Time for the SUS. CHI '13. [PR]** The summary is from recall. A two-item scale ("capabilities meet my requirements" and "easy to use"). *Implication:* the *capabilities* item directly measures perceived capability fit, which is useful for the "didn't need" side. It is also short enough to administer after a drive. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/2470654.2481287)
- **Hone, K.S. & Graham, R. (2000). Towards a Tool for the Subjective Assessment of Speech System Interfaces (SASSI). Natural Language Engineering 6(3–4). [PR, foundational]** The factor names are from recall. The factors are system response accuracy, likeability, cognitive demand, annoyance, *habitability* and speed. Habitability (how well users know what to say and do) is effectively a discoverability measure. It was used in an in-car proactivity study (Schmidt et al. 2020). — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1017/S1351324900002497)
- **Kocaballi, A.B., Laranjo, L., Coiera, E. (2018). Measuring User Experience in Conversational Interfaces: A Comparison of Six Questionnaires. BCS HCI 2018. [PR]** Of six conversational-UX questionnaires, SASSI gave the broadest coverage, while enchantment, playfulness and motivation were under-covered. The authors recommend combining questionnaires. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.14236/ewic/HCI2018.21)
- **Doyle, P.R., Gessinger, I., Edwards, J., Clark, L., Dumbleton, O., Garaialde, D., Rough, D., et al. (2025). The Partner Modelling Questionnaire (PMQ). ACM TOCHI. [PR]** An 18-item semantic-differential scale with three factors (communicative competence and dependability, human-likeness in communication, and communicative flexibility). It has "strong test-retest reliability over 4- and 12-week" intervals. *Implication:* this is the best available instrument for tracking how participants' models of Souffleur's competence change, which is the measurable core of "onboarding as calibration". — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/3729170)
- **Habit**
  - **Verplanken, B. & Orbell, S. (2003). Reflections on Past Behavior: A Self-Report Index of Habit Strength (SRHI). Journal of Applied Social Psychology 33(6). [PR]** A 12-item scale — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1559-1816.2003.tb01951.x).
  - **Gardner, B., Abraham, C., Lally, P., de Bruijn, G.-J. (2012). Towards Parsimony in Habit Measurement: The Self-Report Behavioural Automaticity Index (SRBAI). International Journal of Behavioral Nutrition and Physical Activity 9:102. [PR]** A 4-item automaticity subscale that is "reliable and sensitive" — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1186/1479-5868-9-102).
  - *Implication:* the SRBAI is short enough to administer weekly (e.g., "Starting Souffleur when I drive is something I do automatically").
- **Acceptance and continuance**
  - **Venkatesh, V., Thong, J.Y.L., Xu, X. (2012). Consumer Acceptance and Use of Information Technology: Extending UTAUT (UTAUT2). MIS Quarterly 36(1). [PR]** Adds hedonic motivation, price value and *habit* to UTAUT, and raised explained variance in use from 40% to 52% — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.2307/41410412).
  - Bhattacherjee (2001) ECM and Limayem et al. (2007) are in Q4.
- **Log-derived indicators with precedent**
  - Command length growth and exploration of new topics over time (Bentley et al. 2018) — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/3264901).
  - Obstacle and tactic coding with the categories unfamiliar intent, NLP error, failed feedback and system error (Myers et al. 2018) — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/3173574.3173580).
  - Breakdown and repair coding (Beneteau et al. 2019) — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/3290605.3300473).
  - Early-exploration breadth as a predictor of retention (Zhu et al. 2026, preprint) — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.05767).
- **In-car workload.** The Strayer et al. (2016) DRT-based cognitive workload rating is the standard for whether practice reduces in-car cost — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1186/s41235-016-0018-3).

### Inferences
- A lightweight, field-feasible battery for Souffleur could include the following. Per drive: a single-item ease rating or UMUX-LITE, completed after the drive. Weekly: the SRBAI. At the start, middle and end: the PMQ plus a capability-awareness probe (recall, then recognition). From logs: false-start rate, unsupported-intent attempts, macro-offer acceptance, and the number of distinct capabilities used per week. This aligns with Grossman's split between initial and extended learnability.

### Gaps
- No instrument specifically measures *capability awareness* or *discoverability* for LLM agents. SASSI's habitability factor predates LLMs, and I found no validated LLM-era equivalent.
- The status of the SUS learnability subscale has been questioned, and I did not fetch the later Lewis & Sauro analysis.
- I found no validated scale for *appropriate delegation or offloading* to a conversational agent.

## Q6. Is "onboarding to voice agents" an open gap, and how might it be framed alongside cognitive offloading?

### Takeaway
Yes, with caveats. Discoverability of voice interfaces (2016–2020) and onboarding to generative AI (2024–2026) are both established, but I found no peer-reviewed study of *how people learn an open-ended LLM voice agent in situ, during a primary task, over weeks, without a screen*. I also found none on agent-initiated capability teaching such as macro proposals. The most defensible framing is *onboarding as calibration*: learning what to hand off and what the agent can reliably hold. It bridges the gulf of envisioning, partner models, appropriate reliance and the metacognitive demands of offloading. With ~6 participants it works best as a strong *secondary* contribution: empirically grounded design implications plus a measurement and analysis approach. A full theory of onboarding would be harder to defend.

### Cited Findings
- **Onboarding as calibrating a mental model of the AI's strengths and limits (pre-LLM precedent)**
  - **Cai, C.J., Winter, S., Steiner, D., Wilcox, L., Terry, M. (2019). "Hello AI": Uncovering the Onboarding Needs of Medical Practitioners for Human-AI Collaborative Decision-Making. CSCW (PACM HCI 3). [PR]** Twenty-one pathologists wanted *upfront* information about "basic, global properties of the model, such as its known strengths and limitations, its subjective point-of-view, and its overall design objective". They compared this to forming a mental model of a colleague. *Implication:* this is the canonical "AI onboarding" paper. It shows onboarding is about global capability models, not only about how to issue commands, which is directly transferable to "what can Souffleur hold?". — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/3359206)
  - **Kocielnik, R., Amershi, S., Bennett, P.N. (2019). Will You Accept an Imperfect AI? Exploring Designs for Adjusting End-user Expectations of AI Systems. CHI '19. [PR]** There was "little work on methods for setting appropriate expectations before the initial use". The expectation-adjustment techniques the authors designed significantly increased acceptance of an imperfect AI. *Implication:* this is experimental evidence that pre-use onboarding (setting expectations before the first drive) changes acceptance. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/3290605.3300641)
  - **Amershi, S., Weld, D., Vorvoreanu, M., Fourney, A., Nushi, B., Collisson, P., Suh, J., Iqbal, S., et al. (2019). Guidelines for Human-AI Interaction. CHI '19. [PR, foundational]** The guideline numbers are from recall. Eighteen guidelines validated with 49 practitioners across 20 products. G1 is "make clear what the system can do" and G2 is "make clear how well the system can do what it can do". *Implication:* Souffleur must meet G1 and G2 *by voice*. Doing so is the onboarding problem stated as a design guideline. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/3290605.3300233)
  - **Mozannar, H., Lee, J., Wei, D., Sattigeri, P., Das, S., Sontag, D. (2023). Effective Human-AI Teams via Learned Natural Language Rules and Onboarding. NeurIPS 2023. [PR]** The method learns natural-language rules for when to rely on the AI and teaches them in an onboarding stage, which improved human–AI team accuracy. *Implication:* onboarding can explicitly teach *when to delegate*, which is the offloading framing. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.01007)
- **Offloading and appropriate reliance**
  - **Risko, E.F. & Gilbert, S.J. (2016). Cognitive Offloading. Trends in Cognitive Sciences 20(9). [PR, foundational]** The summary is from recall. Offloading is the use of physical action to reduce internal cognitive demand. Decisions to offload are shaped by *metacognitive* evaluations of one's own abilities relative to the external aid. *Implication:* users must learn the agent's reliability before they will offload to it, so offloading depends on onboarding. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tics.2016.07.002)
  - **Lee, J.D. & See, K.A. (2004). Trust in Automation: Designing for Appropriate Reliance. Human Factors 46(1). [PR, foundational]** The summary is from recall. Appropriate reliance requires trust that is *calibrated* to actual capability. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1518/hfes.46.1.50_30392)
  - Steyvers et al. (2025) show a calibration gap for LLMs, with people over-estimating accuracy — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1038/s42256-024-00976-7).
  - Tankelevitch et al. (2024) describe metacognitive demands of generative AI — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/3613904.3642902).
  - Pradhan et al. (2020) found memory and offloading features underused because of reliability concerns — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/3373759).
- **The closest existing work and why it does not cover Souffleur's case**
  - Kirschthaler et al. 2020: a lab Wizard-of-Oz study of a narrow-domain voice interface.
  - Myers et al. 2018/2019: a lab study of a narrow-domain voice interface.
  - Upadhyay et al. 2023: a retrospective study in a care home with no LLM.
  - Baumann et al. 2025: a training study of commercial voice assistants (details unverified).
  - Gautam et al. 2026: onboarding to generative AI on screen.
  - Shen et al. 2024: text, enterprise.
  - Mahmood et al. 2025/2026: LLM voice assistants, with a focus on breakdowns rather than learning.
  - In-car LLM papers (Du 2024; Mathis 2024; Susak 2026; Hamid 2026): proactivity *timing and acceptance*, not capability learning, and run in simulators.

### Inferences
- **Candidate novelty claim (inference).** Souffleur would be the first in-situ, multi-week study of how people come to understand, and calibrate their reliance on, an LLM voice agent used during a safety-critical primary task. In that setting, capability learning cannot rely on visual feedforward and has to happen through (a) the agent's own talk (proactive offers, macro proposals, answers to "what can you do?") and (b) review before and after the drive.
- **Framing that ties the two contributions (inference).** The offloading contribution answers *what people hand off while driving*. The onboarding contribution answers *how they learn what they can hand off*: a calibration process visible in false starts, unsupported-intent attempts, silences read as failure, and acceptance or rejection of macro offers. A possible phrase is "learning to offload". It links to Risko & Gilbert (metacognitive offloading decisions), Lee & See (calibrated reliance), Subramonyam (envisioning) and Cai (onboarding as forming a global model of the AI's strengths and limits).
- **Risks to the claim (inference).**
  1. With N≈6 and most participants making 1–9 drives, evidence of *extended* learnability will be thin. The contribution is safer framed as "onboarding breakdowns and design implications" than as "how learning unfolds".
  2. Reviewers may say the findings restate known voice-interface discoverability problems (Luger & Sellen, Myers, Kirschthaler). The defence is what is LLM- and agent-specific: fluent responses hiding failed actions (Mahmood 2025), proactive and macro offers as discovery, and calibration of "what it holds".
  3. The data must separate onboarding failure from engineering failure. Many of the 18-second false starts may be technical rather than about learnability.
  4. The field moves fast. A targeted ACM DL and arXiv sweep just before submission is advisable because 2026 work may have appeared since this scan.

### Gaps
- I could not verify whether any CHI 2026, CSCW 2026 or IMWUT 2026 paper (beyond those listed) studies onboarding to LLM voice agents specifically. My searches found none, but coverage of mid-to-late 2026 proceedings is incomplete.
- No industry or peer-reviewed data was found on how users of Alexa+, Gemini Live or ChatGPT Voice discover agentic capabilities.
- I found no study of voice agents that *propose* reusable automations or macros, or of whether users understand and adopt them.
- I could not confirm the details of Baumann et al. 2025 (training effects), which may be the closest quantitative onboarding study for voice assistants.
